Most people learn anagrams first. That is the wrong order.

When we analysed thousands of real cryptic clues, the **charade** — not the anagram — was the single most common device, turning up in roughly **2 clues out of every 5**. If you want the fastest improvement in your solving, this is the trick to master first.

The good news: charades are also the most *logical* device. There is no jumbling, no hidden trickery — you simply build the answer by joining smaller pieces in order, like beads on a string.

<Callout>
**In one sentence:** a charade clue defines the answer once, then defines its parts separately — and you stitch those parts together. *Evening close from SUN + SET (6)* → SUN + SET → **SUNSET**.
</Callout>

## What is a charade clue?

A **charade** builds the answer from two or more pieces placed side by side. The clue gives you a **definition** of the whole answer (at the start or end), plus separate clues to each **piece**. Join the pieces in order and you have the solution.

The name comes from the old parlour game where players act out a word one syllable at a time — same idea, in letters.

Unlike an anagram (which announces itself with an indicator like "mixed") or a container (signalled by "about"), **charades often have no indicator at all**. The pieces are simply laid out in reading order. That is what makes them feel clean once you spot the pattern — and slightly sneaky until you do.

## Why learn charades first?

Because they are everywhere. [Our analysis of 5,712 clues](/blog/cryptic-crossword-statistics) found charades in **39.7%** of them — ahead of anagrams at 19.9%. Setters and editors have long said "around a third of clues are pure or part charade," and the data backs that up.

So the maths is simple: get good at charades and you unlock the largest slice of any puzzle.

## The three ways each piece is clued

Here is the one thing that turns charades from confusing to obvious. Each piece of the answer is clued in one of just three ways:

![Three ways a charade clues each piece: abbreviation (S + TART = START), synonym (HAND + SOME = HANDSOME), literal (SUN + SET = SUNSET)](/images/charade-three-ways.svg)

- **An abbreviation or single letter** — "small" → S, "navy" → RN, "you" → U. So *S + TART* spells **START**.
- **A synonym** — "give" → HAND, "a few" → SOME. So *HAND + SOME* spells **HANDSOME**.
- **The literal word** — the clue hands you the piece outright, as in *SUN + SET* → **SUNSET**.

Most charades mix these. The hardest ones use all synonyms, because you have to translate every piece before you can even see the build.

## Worked examples, from easy to tricky

Let's solve four real clues from our archive, building difficulty as we go.

**1. Literal pieces — the gentlest kind**

> *Evening close from SUN + SET (6)*

The definition is "Evening close". The clue literally names the parts: SUN + SET. Join them → **SUNSET**. When a clue spells out "X + Y", it is doing the assembly for you.

**2. A letter plus a word**

> *Step to READ with T in front (5)*

Definition: "Step". Take READ and put T in front → T + READ → **TREAD**. The phrase "in front" is a *position indicator*: it tells you where the piece goes.

**3. Synonym pieces — the real test**

> *Vehicle carrying animal makes floor covering (6)*

Definition: "floor covering". Now nothing is spelled out — you must translate. "Vehicle" → CAR, "animal" → PET. CAR + PET → **CARPET**. This is the leap that separates beginners from solvers: seeing that ordinary words like "vehicle" and "animal" are really pointing at CAR and PET.

**4. Two synonyms hiding in a sentence**

> *Listen to the call for jewellery (7)*

Definition: "jewellery". "Listen" → EAR, "the call" → RING. EAR + RING → **EARRING**. The surface reads like a coherent sentence, which is exactly the misdirection — wipe the surface meaning and ask what each word *could* stand for.

## Position words: the charade's only indicators

Charades usually have no indicator, but when the pieces are clued out of order, the setter signals it with a small **position word**:

- **Before / after / following** — fixes the order of two pieces.
- **In front of / leading / heading** — puts a piece at the start.
- **On / over / above** (in Down clues) — one piece sits atop another.
- **With / and / by** — neutral joiners that simply say "next to".

Spotting these tells you how to arrange the pieces once you've identified them. For the full data on which words signal each device, see our [indicator-words study](/blog/cryptic-crossword-indicator-words).

## How to spot (and crack) a charade

A quick routine:

1. **Find the definition** at the start or end of the clue.
2. **Look at what's left.** If there's no anagram, container or hidden indicator, suspect a charade — the default device.
3. **Break the remainder into 2–3 chunks** and ask, for each: is it an abbreviation, a synonym, or a literal word?
4. **Join the chunks** in the order given (watch for position words) and check the result against the definition and the letter count.

<Aside>
**The mindset shift:** charades reward you for distrusting the surface. "Vehicle carrying animal" is not about traffic — it's CAR + PET. Train yourself to read every word as a possible abbreviation or synonym, not as part of a sentence.
</Aside>

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a charade clue in a cryptic crossword?**
A charade builds the answer from smaller pieces joined in order. The clue defines the whole answer plus each piece separately — for example, "Evening close from SUN + SET (6)" gives SUN + SET = SUNSET. It is the most common cryptic device, in roughly 40% of clues.

**Do charade clues have indicators?**
Usually not. Unlike anagrams or containers, charades often place their pieces in plain reading order with no signal word. When the order is unusual, a position word like "before", "after" or "in front" shows how the pieces fit together.

**Why are charades considered the easiest cryptic clue type to start with?**
Because they are logical — you join pieces rather than jumble or hide them — and because they are the most common, so practice transfers to the largest share of clues. Two-part charades with literal pieces are the gentlest entry point.

**How do I get better at spotting charade pieces?**
Practise translating ordinary words into abbreviations and synonyms: "small" → S, "vehicle" → CAR, "navy" → RN. The more of these you internalise, the faster you'll see that a clue's surface story is really a stack of hidden parts.
