Music gives cryptic setters more raw material than almost any other subject. Sheet music is already written in code: single letters for loudness, seven letters for the notes, and Italian terms most of us half-remember from school. Setters borrow all of it. Learn a handful of conventions and clues that looked like nonsense turn into simple letter sums.

It works the other way round too. Plenty of everyday words, such as organ, bar, score and key, have a musical meaning a setter can lean on to send you in the wrong direction. This guide covers both sides: the musical shorthand that builds answers, and the musical-sounding words that are only there to distract you.

<Callout>
**The core idea:** a musical word in a clue usually does one of two jobs. Either it stands for a few letters ("soft" = P, "note" = any letter from A to G), or it is a decoy whose musical meaning is the wrong one ("organ" is often just an ear). Work out which job it is doing before you go any further.
</Callout>

## The Musical Shorthand Setters Use

Here are the musical words you will meet most often, with the letters they usually stand for. The middle column shows where each one comes from.

![Musical crossword shorthand: soft = P (piano), very soft = PP, loud = F (forte), very loud = FF, note = A to G, note = DOH or TE in sol-fa, record = LP or EP, tune = AIR, song = LAY, singer = ALTO, composer = ARNE, instrument = OBOE](/images/music-crossword-words.svg)

### Dynamics: P and F

This is the most useful pair on the list. In printed music, *p* (piano) tells the player to play softly and *f* (forte) tells them to play loudly. Setters treat both as standard abbreviations:

- **soft, quiet, softly, quietly** → P
- **loud, loudly** → F
- **very soft** or **very quiet** → PP, and **very loud** → FF

"Soft" and "quiet" are such ordinary adjectives that they vanish into a surface. A clue that mentions a soft spot, a quiet word or a loud shirt may well be handing you a single letter.

### Notes: A to G, and doh to te

"Note" is one of the most flexible words a setter has. In the letter system it can be any of A, B, C, D, E, F or G, which is a gift when the setter needs one stray letter to finish an answer. "Key" often works the same way.

In tonic sol-fa the notes become syllables: DOH (or DO), RAY (RE), ME (MI), FAH (FA), SOH (SO or SOL), LAH (LA) and TE (TI). TE and ME turn up constantly because they slot neatly into longer words. Take *"Note a capital (4)"*: DOH plus A gives **DOHA**, the capital of Qatar.

### Records, songs and singers

- **record** → LP, EP or DISC
- **tune** or **song** → AIR, LAY, ARIA or DITTY (and "number" can mean a song, too)
- **singer** → ALTO, BASS, TENOR or DIVA
- **composer** → ARNE, BACH, ELGAR or IVES. Thomas Arne, who wrote "Rule, Britannia!", is a crossword regular purely because his name has such friendly letters
- **instrument** → OBOE, HARP, LUTE or ORGAN

You will find these alongside the wider set of single-letter codes in our [cryptic abbreviations](/cryptic-abbreviations) reference.

## How a Dynamic Hides in a Clue

![A worked example: "Soft drink looks wan (4)" — "soft" gives P, "drink" gives ALE, and together they make PALE, which means wan](/images/music-clue-example.svg)

Take *"Soft drink looks wan (4)"*. On the surface it is about lemonade. Read cryptically, "soft drink" splits in two: "soft" is P and "drink" is ALE. Put them together and you get **PALE**, which is what "wan" means. "Looks" is simply the link word between the wordplay and the definition.

That is a charade, the most common clue type of all, and our guide to [solving charade clues](/blog/how-to-solve-charade-clues) walks through the method step by step. The same trick builds dozens of answers:

- *"Loud hideout reveals natural talent (5)"*: F (loud) + LAIR (hideout) = **FLAIR**
- *"Soft organ yields fruit (4)"*: P (soft) + EAR (organ) = **PEAR**

Look at what "organ" is doing in that second clue. It sits right next to "soft", so your mind reaches for church music, but here it is a body part. Which brings us to the decoys.

## Words That Only Sound Musical

A surprising number of English words have a musical sense and a completely unrelated one. Setters love them, because one word can pull the surface towards the concert hall while the answer lives somewhere else entirely.

| Clue word | Musical sense | What it may really mean |
| --- | --- | --- |
| organ | instrument | ear, eye or heart; a newspaper |
| bass | low voice | a fish |
| bar | a measure of music | a pub; except |
| score | written music | twenty |
| key | the pitch of a piece | an island; crucial |
| staff | the five lines | employees |
| pitch | how high a note sounds | a sports field; tar; to throw |
| flat / sharp | lowered / raised note | an apartment / clever |
| refrain | a chorus | to hold back |
| forte | loud | a strong point |

Several of these make perfect double definitions, where a clue simply gives two meanings of the same word. *"Low singer or fish (4)"* is **BASS**, and *"Hold back chorus (7)"* is **REFRAIN**. Our list of [misleading words](/cryptic-misleading-words) covers the same habit well beyond music, and our [zodiac words guide](/blog/zodiac-words-in-cryptic-crosswords) does it for the star signs.

## Learn the Notes the Way the Song Does

The best-known lesson in sol-fa is practically a set of crossword clues. In *The Sound of Music*, "Do-Re-Mi" teaches each syllable by pairing it with an everyday word that sounds the same: RAY becomes a beam of sunlight, ME is what you call yourself, SEW is what a needle does, and TEA is something you drink. That is homophone wordplay, exactly the kind we explain in our [guide to homophone clues](/blog/how-to-solve-homophone-clues). It is also why the song works so well as a lesson: a tune makes a list stick far better than reading it ever will.

You can use the same trick on any list in this article. String the dynamics and sol-fa notes into a few lines of lyrics, or gather your favourite clue surfaces into a verse, and set them to music. You don't need to play anything yourself: [Tunora's AI music generator](https://tunora.io/) turns pasted lyrics into a full song with vocals in whatever genre you choose, so "soft is P, loud is F" can become a chorus you won't forget. It also makes a fun extra for a puzzle club, a newsletter, or anyone who solves on stream and fancies a theme tune.

## Practise Listening for Music

Next time a clue mentions something soft, loud, quiet or a note, pause before you take it literally. It may be a single letter in disguise, or a musical decoy covering for a pub, a fish or a body part.
