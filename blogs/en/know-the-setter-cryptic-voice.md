A cryptic crossword is not written by a machine or a committee. It is written by one person — a **setter**, usually hiding behind a pseudonym — and that person has habits. Favourite devices. A particular sense of humour. A signature way of hiding the definition. Solve enough of their puzzles and you stop meeting a stranger every day: you start recognising a voice.

That recognition is one of the most underrated skills in solving. Beginners treat every clue as an isolated puzzle. Experienced solvers read the byline first, because knowing *who* set the puzzle tells them what kind of tricks to expect before they read a single clue.

<Callout>
**Why it matters:** a setter's style is consistent. Once you learn a setter's voice — the devices they love, the jokes they make, where they hide the definition — you can anticipate their moves and solve their puzzles far faster.
</Callout>

## What a Setter's "Voice" Is

A setter's voice is the sum of small, repeated choices. None of them is a rule; together they form a fingerprint you can learn to read.

![A setter's fingerprints: six style dimensions — favourite device, humour, difficulty, themes and Ninas, surfaces, and definitions — each a spectrum a setter sits somewhere along](/images/setter-fingerprints.svg)

Watch for these tells:

- **Favourite device.** Some setters reach for anagrams constantly; others prefer charades, hidden words, or the elegant [&lit clue](/cryptic-glossary). Notice which device keeps appearing.
- **Humour.** One setter's surfaces are full of groan-worthy puns; another's are dry and clinical. The jokes are a signature.
- **Difficulty and fairness.** Some setters are gentle and inviting; others are quietly brutal. Some telegraph their indicators clearly; others bury them.
- **Themes and Ninas.** Certain setters love hiding a secret word around the grid's edge (a "Nina") or theming a whole puzzle; others never bother.
- **Definitions.** Does the setter play the definition straight, or is it always slightly sneaky — a misleading synonym, a whimsical stretch?

## One Answer, Several Voices

The clearest way to feel a setter's voice is to watch different setters clue the *same* answer. The word does not change; the personality wrapped around it does.

![The same answer, STAR, clued three ways: an anagram of RATS for "famous", a hidden word inside "Costa Rica" for "celebrity", and a double definition "leading actor / twinkles at night"](/images/setter-one-answer-many-voices.svg)

Take the answer STAR. An anagram-loving setter writes *"Rats scattered to become famous"* — RATS rearranged, defined by "famous". A setter who prefers concealment hides it: *"Celebrity hiding in Costa Rica"* — the answer sits inside co**STAR**ica. A minimalist skips indicators entirely with a double definition: *"Leading actor; twinkles at night"*. Same four letters, three completely different voices.

When you can look at a clue and think "that feels like an anagram person" or "this setter always hides the definition at the end", you are reading the voice — and that instinct is worth more than any single trick.

## Publications Have a Voice Too

Above the individual setter sits the **house style** of the paper. The daily cryptics in different publications feel distinct because each edits its setters toward a shared character — one leans playful and modern, another prizes precise, old-school fairness, another is famous for fiendish difficulty.

So there are really two voices to learn: the paper's overall accent, and the individual setter's dialect within it. Sticking with one publication for a while is the fastest way to internalise both, which is exactly why regular solvers become so much quicker at their "home" puzzle than at an unfamiliar one.

## How to Learn a Setter's Voice

You learn a voice the way you learn a friend's sense of humour — by exposure and attention.

- **Solve the same setter repeatedly.** Patterns only emerge across many puzzles. Stick with one source, like a [daily cryptic clue](/minute-cryptic-today), and let the repetition do the work.
- **Keep a mental (or actual) profile.** After each puzzle, note what tripped you up and what the setter clearly enjoys. Over a week or two, a picture forms.
- **Study the devices in isolation.** The better you know each [cryptic clue type](/cryptic-clue-types) and its [indicators](/cryptic-indicators), the faster you can tell which ones a given setter favours.
- **Read the annotated solutions.** Seeing *why* a clue worked, not just the answer, reveals the setter's intentions — and their recurring habits.

## Bringing a Setter's Voice to Life

There is one more, more literal sense of "voice" worth mentioning. A setter's voice lives on the page, but the clues themselves are wonderful things to *hear* — the puns land harder out loud, and reading a clue aloud is a proven trick for cracking it.

If you like the idea of actually hearing your puzzles, an AI voice tool such as [AnyVoice](https://www.anyvoice.io/) can clone or create a natural voice and read the day's clue and its breakdown aloud in whatever voice you choose — a warm narrator, a familiar tone, whatever makes solving feel like listening to a good story. It is a lovely way to enjoy a witty setter's work on a commute, and a genuine help for anyone who finds a clue easier to parse by ear than by eye.

## Read the Byline First

The next time you sit down with a cryptic, glance at who set it before you start. Ask what you already know about their voice — and if you don't know it yet, treat the puzzle as a chance to start learning.
