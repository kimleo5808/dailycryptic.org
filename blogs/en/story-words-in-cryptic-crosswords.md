Cryptic setters are writers, so it is no surprise that the vocabulary of books and storytelling runs all through their clues. "Story", "novel", "chapter", "page" and "the writer" all look like plain literary words, and each one has a second job that can unlock an answer in seconds once you know it.

The literary world also gives setters a cast of reliable characters. A handful of names from Shakespeare, Dickens and the Brontës turn up again and again, because they are short, well known and made of friendly letters. This guide covers both: the story words that are really codes, and the characters worth knowing by heart.

<Callout>
**The core idea:** when a clue talks about stories, books or writers, assume nothing is literal. "Story" might be a LIE, "books" might be NT, "novel" might mean "rearrange these letters", and "the writer" is probably the setter talking about themselves.
</Callout>

## The Shorthand Hiding in Book Words

Here are the codes and characters you will meet most often. The left column turns ordinary book words into letters; the right column lists the fictional characters setters lean on.

![Story shorthand in cryptic clues: story = TALE or LIE, book = B, books = NT or OT, chapter = CH, page = P, the writer = ME or I; and stock characters: tragic king = LEAR, villain = IAGO, governess = EYRE, captain = AHAB, orphan = PIP, sprite = ARIEL](/images/story-crossword-words.svg)

### "Story" is rarely just a story

On its own, "story" usually means **TALE**, but a story can also be a **LIE** (telling stories is fibbing), a **YARN**, a **SAGA**, a **MYTH** or an **EPIC**. American spelling adds one more: a story is also a floor of a building, so **FLOOR**, **LEVEL** and **TIER** are all fair game.

Position words work too. "End of story" is the last letter, **Y**, and "story's opening" is **S**. These are the same first-and-last-letter tricks you see everywhere in cryptics, dressed up in literary clothes.

### Books, chapters and pages

Printed books come with their own abbreviations, and setters use every one:

- **book** → B, and **volume** → VOL
- **books** → NT or OT, for the New and Old Testaments
- **chapter** → CH or C
- **page** → P, and **line** → L
- **edition** or **editor** → ED

"Books" deserves special mention. It almost never means books in general; when it appears in wordplay it is nearly always handing you NT or OT. You can find these alongside hundreds of other codes in our [cryptic abbreviations](/cryptic-abbreviations) reference.

### The writer is the setter

When a clue says "the writer", "this writer", "the author" or "yours truly", it usually means the setter, and so it gives **I** or **ME**. It is one of the oldest conventions in the cryptic crossword, and one reason setters develop such a recognisable voice; our piece on [knowing the setter's voice](/blog/know-the-setter-cryptic-voice) explores that side of things.

### "Novel" means rearrange

As a definition, "novel" can simply mean **NEW**. In wordplay, though, it is a classic anagram indicator: a novel arrangement of letters. In *"Novel Lear produces a peer (4)"*, "novel" tells you to rearrange LEAR, which gives **EARL**, a peer. Our [indicator words study](/blog/cryptic-crossword-indicator-words) shows how often signals like this appear.

## How a Story Hides in a Clue

![A worked example: "Story books show gift (6)" — "story" gives TALE, "books" gives NT, and together they make TALENT, which means gift](/images/story-clue-example.svg)

Take *"Story books show gift (6)"*. On the surface it sounds like a child unwrapping picture books. Cryptically, "story" is TALE and "books" is NT, the New Testament. Join them and you get **TALENT**, which is what "gift" means. "Show" is just the link word. That is a charade, the most common clue type of all, and our guide to [solving charade clues](/blog/how-to-solve-charade-clues) walks through the method.

## The Characters Setters Keep Casting

Some fictional names appear so often that experienced solvers write them in almost without thinking:

- **LEAR**: Shakespeare's tragic king, and also Edward Lear of nonsense verse. Its letters are an anagram of EARL and REAL, which helps explain its popularity.
- **IAGO**: the villain of *Othello*, and a gift to setters with all those vowels.
- **EYRE**: Jane Eyre, the governess of Charlotte Brontë's novel.
- **AHAB**: the obsessed captain of *Moby-Dick*.
- **PIP**: the orphan hero of *Great Expectations*. A pip is also a seed, so *"Dickens hero's seed (3)"* is a neat double definition.
- **ARIEL**: the airy spirit of *The Tempest*.
- **HAMLET**: the Danish prince is also a small village, as in *"Danish prince or small village (6)"*.

Like the zodiac signs in our [zodiac words guide](/blog/zodiac-words-in-cryptic-crosswords), these names are worth learning in both directions: a clue may define the character, or it may use the character to define something else.

## Book Words That Mislead

Many words from the library have a second meaning that has nothing to do with reading, and that second meaning is often the one a setter wants.

| Clue word | Literary sense | What it may really mean |
| --- | --- | --- |
| novel | a book-length story | new; an anagram indicator |
| yarn | a tall tale | wool or thread |
| plot | the storyline | a patch of land; to scheme |
| book | something to read | to reserve; B |
| volume | a single book | loudness; VOL |
| chapter | part of a book | a branch of a society; CH |
| page | a leaf of a book | a young attendant; P |
| character | a person in a story | a letter; an eccentric |
| legend | an old story | a caption or map key; a star performer |
| tale | a story | sounds like TAIL |

That last row is a homophone waiting to happen: "tale, we hear" can give TAIL, and our [guide to homophone clues](/blog/how-to-solve-homophone-clues) shows how setters signal the swap. For the wider habit of ordinary words hiding unexpected meanings, see our list of [misleading words](/cryptic-misleading-words).

## Every Clue Is a Tiny Story

Good setters do more than hide an answer. They write a surface reading that makes sense as a sentence, and the best surfaces are miniature stories: a child unwrapping books, a king making his heir a peer, a captain chasing a whale. Learning to see past that little story to the wordplay underneath is the central skill of solving, and our guide to [surface reading](/blog/surface-reading-cryptic-clues) covers it in depth.

You can also play the game the other way round. Take the surface of today's clue and treat it as the first line of a story: who is speaking, and what happens next? It makes a good warm-up for a writing group, or a classroom activity that slips some wordplay into an English lesson. If you want a head start, paste the surface into an [AI story generator like Story-AI](https://story-ai.org/), pick a genre and a length, and it will spin that one sentence into a full short story in seconds. Comparing what the story makes of the words with what the setter actually meant is a neat lesson in how differently the same sentence can be read, and that double reading is the whole art of the cryptic clue.

## Practise Reading Between the Lines

Next time a clue mentions a story, a book, a page or the writer, stop and ask which job the word is really doing. It may be a code for a few letters, an anagram signal, or a character who has been waiting in the wings all along.
