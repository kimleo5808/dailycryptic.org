Long before anyone set a cryptic crossword, people were writing words with pictures. A drawing of an eye stood for "I", a bee for "be", and the Bowes-Lyon family put bows and lions on its coat of arms. That kind of picture pun is called a rebus, from the Latin for "by things": the message is spelled out with objects instead of letters.

If you solve cryptics, rebus puzzles will feel oddly familiar. A picture of a car next to a picture of a cat spells CARPET in exactly the way a cryptic charade does. Words written backwards, tucked inside each other or stacked on top of one another work just like reversal, container and positional clues. Once you see the connection, each kind of puzzle makes you better at the other.

<Callout>
**The core idea:** a rebus and a cryptic clue both build an answer out of pieces. The rebus shows the pieces as pictures or page layouts; the cryptic clue hides them inside words. Car + pet = CARPET either way.
</Callout>

## Three Kinds of Rebus, Three Kinds of Clue

### Picture sums are charades

The simplest rebus puts pictures side by side and asks you to read them in order. A car and a pet make CARPET; a key and a board make KEYBOARD.

That is a charade, the cryptic clue type that joins pieces end to end. *"Motor and favourite rug (6)"* gives CAR + PET = **CARPET**. The only difference is that the setter hides each piece behind a synonym ("motor" for CAR, "favourite" for PET) instead of drawing it. Our guide to [solving charade clues](/blog/how-to-solve-charade-clues) covers the method in full.

### Sound-alike pictures are homophones

Many rebus pictures work by sound rather than spelling. An eye is "I", a bee is "be", a ewe is "you" and the sea is "see". Put a bee next to a leaf and you get BELIEF, which looks nothing like either picture on paper. Text speak runs on the same idea: "CU L8R" and "IOU" are rebuses most of us write without thinking.

Cryptic setters use homophones in exactly this way, usually flagged with phrases like "we hear" or "they say". *"Spot the ocean, we hear (3)"* is **SEE**, because it sounds like "sea". Our [guide to homophone clues](/blog/how-to-solve-homophone-clues) lists the common signals.

### Layout puzzles are positional clues

The third kind uses no pictures at all, just words arranged on the page. These are often called dingbats or wordies: MAN written above BOARD reads "man overboard", and MIL1LION reads "one in a million". Every one of them depends on where one word sits relative to another, and that is exactly what cryptic indicators like "over", "in", "back" and "after" describe.

## Six Dingbats and the Clue Device Behind Each

![Six dingbats and their cryptic devices: MAN above BOARD is man overboard ("over"), MIL1LION is one in a million (container), ECNALG is backward glance (reversal), DEATH LIFE is life after death (word order), READING between two lines is reading between the lines (insertion), and STAND above I is I understand ("under")](/images/rebus-dingbats.svg)

Each of these has a direct cryptic equivalent:

- **Over and under.** In a down clue, where the answer reads top to bottom, "A over B" puts A on top. MAN over BOARD and I under STAND are the same idea drawn on the page.
- **In and between.** MIL1LION puts one thing inside another, which is a container clue. *"Artist in bin shows intelligence (5)"* puts RA (a Royal Academician, so an artist) inside BIN to give **BRAIN**.
- **Back.** ECNALG is a glance written backwards. Reversal indicators such as "back", "returning" and "turning" do the same job: *"Celebrity turning into rodents (4)"* reverses STAR to give **RATS**.
- **After.** DEATH LIFE puts life after death. In a cryptic clue, "A after B" means write B first, then A.

If you can read a dingbat, you already understand the logic behind a large share of cryptic indicators. Our list of [cryptic indicators](/cryptic-indicators) groups them by job, and our [indicator words study](/blog/cryptic-crossword-indicator-words) shows which ones setters reach for most.

## Same Answer, Two Puzzles

![A picture rebus of a car plus a cat spells CARPET, and the cryptic clue "Motor and favourite rug (6)" builds the same answer from "motor" = CAR and "favourite" = PET](/images/rebus-clue-example.svg)

Put a rebus next to a cryptic clue for the same answer and the overlap is plain. The picture version shows a car and a cat; the cryptic version says "motor" and "favourite". Both ask you to name each piece and join them up. The cryptic clue adds two extra layers: a definition ("rug") that confirms you have the right answer, and a surface reading designed to make you think about something else entirely.

That is why rebus puzzles are such a good way in for new solvers, children especially. They teach the central habit of cryptics, which is to stop reading a clue as a sentence and start reading it as a set of building blocks, without needing any of the vocabulary a cryptic demands. If you are new to cryptics yourself, our [beginner's guide](/cryptic-crossword-for-beginners) picks up from there.

## Make Your Own Picture Rebus

Rebus puzzles used to need someone who could draw. Now you can take any charade you like, split it into its pieces and turn each piece into a picture. Describe each one in plain words, such as "a small red cartoon car on a white background" or "a sleeping tabby cat, flat illustration", and an [AI image generator like Anyscene](https://anyscene.app/) will produce it in whatever style you pick, from flat cartoon to watercolour. Set the pictures side by side with a plus sign between them and you have a rebus.

A few answers that work well as picture puzzles:

- **CARPET**: a car and a pet
- **EARRING**: an ear and a ring
- **BUTTERFLY**: a pat of butter and a fly
- **KIDNAP**: a young goat (a kid) taking a nap
- **BELIEF**: a bee and a leaf, which only works out loud

They make quick warm-ups for a quiz night, a classroom starter or a family puzzle sheet. Building them is also a surprisingly good way to practise thinking like a setter, because you have to break a word into pieces before you can draw it.

## Practise Seeing the Pieces

Next time a cryptic clue has you stuck, try picturing it as a rebus. Which pieces would you draw, and in what order or position? Often the layout is the answer.
