Most cryptic clues split neatly in two: a definition at one end and wordplay filling the rest. Once you learn that rule, you can take almost any clue apart.

Then you meet a clue that refuses to split. Either the whole clue is wordplay and the whole clue is also the definition, or there is no wordplay at all, just one sly description. These are **&lit clues** and **cryptic definitions**, the two clue types that break the usual rules. They are rarer than anagrams and charades, but they are often the clues people remember.

**What is an &lit clue?** An &lit clue is a cryptic clue in which the entire sentence works twice: once as wordplay that builds the answer and once, read literally, as the definition. The name is short for "and literally so". There is no separate definition and no spare words.

**What is a cryptic definition?** A cryptic definition is a clue that consists of a single misleading description of the answer, with no wordplay at all. It relies on a pun or a second meaning, and it usually ends with a question mark.

- How &lit clues, semi-&lits and cryptic definitions differ from ordinary clues

## Where These Clues Fit

It helps to see all the shapes a cryptic clue can take side by side.

![Four shapes of cryptic clue: a standard clue has a definition at one end and wordplay in the rest; a double definition has two definitions; an &lit has the whole clue acting as both wordplay and definition; a cryptic definition has the whole clue as one misleading definition with no wordplay](/images/blog/and-lit-clues/clue-shapes.webp)

| Clue shape | Definition | Wordplay | Usual punctuation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard clue | At one end | The rest of the clue | Anything |
| Double definition | Two of them | None | Anything |
| &lit | The whole clue | The whole clue | Often "!" |
| Semi-&lit | The whole clue | Part of the clue | Often "!" or "?" |
| Cryptic definition | The whole clue | None | Usually "?" |

The two rule-breakers sit at opposite extremes. An &lit has *more* going on than a normal clue, because every word does two jobs. A cryptic definition has *less*, because there is no wordplay to check.

## &lit Clues: When Every Word Works Twice

In an ordinary clue, some words build the answer and some words define it. In an &lit, those are the same words. You read the clue once as instructions, then again as a plain description, and both readings arrive at the same answer.

Here is an example written for this guide:

> **Dirty room, perhaps (9)**
> As wordplay: DIRTY ROOM is anagrammed ("perhaps" signals the rearrangement) to give **DORMITORY**.
> As a definition: the whole clue describes a dormitory, which might well be a dirty room.

![An &lit clue read two ways: "Dirty room, perhaps (9)" read as wordplay rearranges the letters of DIRTY ROOM into DORMITORY, and read as a plain description defines a dormitory as a possibly dirty room](/images/blog/and-lit-clues/two-readings.webp)

Notice that there is nothing left over. In a standard clue you could cover the definition and still see wordplay. In an &lit, covering any word breaks both readings at once.

### More &lit examples

Anagrams are the most common route to an &lit, because some phrases happen to be rearrangements of the thing they describe. Two more examples written for this guide:

> **Moon starer, perhaps (10)**
> MOON STARER rearranged gives **ASTRONOMER**, and an astronomer is literally a moon starer.

> **They see, moving about (3,4)**
> THEY SEE rearranged gives **THE EYES**, and the eyes are what see, moving about as they do.

Anagrams are not the only route. Here is an &lit built from first letters:

> **Initially the eye's agitated response (4)**
> The first letters of **T**he **E**ye's **A**gitated **R**esponse spell **TEAR**, and a tear is exactly the eye's response when it is agitated.

📝 **Note:** "perhaps" is a mild anagram indicator that stricter setters avoid. In an &lit it does double duty, softening the definition ("a dormitory is perhaps a dirty room") while signalling the anagram. That kind of economy is what makes &lits admired.

### Why &lit clues are marked with an exclamation mark

Many setters end an &lit with an exclamation mark, as a quiet signal to read the clue as a whole. It is a convention rather than a rule: some editors prefer a question mark, and some use no mark at all. When you see a clue ending in "!", it is always worth asking whether the whole sentence might be the definition.

## Semi-&lits: Halfway There

A **semi-&lit** is a clue where the whole sentence is the definition, but only part of it is the wordplay. The extra words support the definition without contributing letters.

> **Stressed? Turn back to these! (8)** — *example written for this guide*
> As wordplay: "stressed", turned back, gives **DESSERTS**.
> As a definition: the whole clue describes desserts as the comfort food people turn to when stressed.

The words "to these" are part of the definition reading but play no role in the wordplay. That is what makes it a semi-&lit rather than a full &lit.

Many solvers use "semi-&lit" loosely for any clue where the definition and wordplay overlap. The important point for solving is the same: the definition may be spread across the whole clue rather than sitting neatly at one end.

## Cryptic Definitions: All Definition, No Wordplay

A cryptic definition goes the other way. Instead of doubling up, it strips the clue down to one misleading description. There is no anagram, no hidden word and no charade, just a definition that reads one way and means another.

Some examples written for this guide:

> **Flower of London? (6)**
> A "flower" here is something that flows. London's flower is the **THAMES**.

> **One managing two banks? (5)**
> It sounds like a financier, but a **RIVER** has two banks, one on each side.

> **Number used in surgery? (11)**
> A "number" here is something that numbs: an **ANAESTHETIC** (British spelling).

> **Hold-up specialists? (6)**
> Not bank robbers but **BRACES**, which hold up trousers in British English. Americans call them suspenders.

![How a cryptic definition misleads: "Number used in surgery? (11)" first reads as a figure or count, but "number" re-read as numb-er, something that numbs, gives ANAESTHETIC](/images/blog/and-lit-clues/misdirection.webp)

The whole trick lives in one word read the wrong way: "flower", "banks", "number", "hold-up". Once you find that word and re-read it, the answer usually arrives at once.

### Cryptic definitions versus double definitions

Both have no wordplay, so they are easy to confuse. A **double definition** gives two separate, straight definitions of the same answer, as in "Elephant's nose and a large case (5)" for TRUNK, from our archive. A **cryptic definition** gives one definition, deliberately phrased to mislead. If you can split the clue into two honest meanings, it is a double definition. If it is one meaning wrapped in a pun, it is a cryptic definition.

## How to Recognise These Clues

None of these clue types announces itself, but they leave clues of their own.

### Signs of an &lit

- The clue ends in an exclamation mark.
- You have found complete wordplay, but no words are left over to be the definition.
- The whole sentence, read plainly, describes your answer.

### Signs of a cryptic definition

- The clue ends in a question mark.
- The clue is short and contains no indicator words at all.
- One word seems strangely chosen, as if it has a second meaning.
- The surface reads like a riddle rather than an instruction.

### Signs that it is neither

- One end of the clue is a clean synonym and the rest is clearly wordplay.
- You can point to an indicator such as "mixed", "hidden in" or "we hear".

Most clues, of course, are neither. In our own library of 1,004 daily clues, every clue uses one of the eight standard devices. Short daily puzzles rarely use &lits or pure cryptic definitions, because they are hard to make fair in a single clue with no crossing letters.

## How to Solve Them

The strategy differs for each type.

### Solving an &lit

1. **Solve it as ordinary wordplay first.** Look for an indicator and fodder, just as you would in any clue.
2. **When the wordplay is complete, look for a definition.** If none of the remaining words is a definition, because there are no remaining words, you are probably looking at an &lit.
3. **Read the whole clue as a description.** Check that it describes the answer you have built. If it does, you are done.

&lits are often easier than they look, because the wordplay is complete and self-checking. The hard part is believing that the whole clue really is the definition.

### Solving a cryptic definition

1. **Assume the obvious reading is wrong.** The surface meaning is the trap.
2. **Re-read each word for a second meaning.** Pay special attention to nouns that could be verbs ("flower"), and words that can be split ("number" as numb-er).
3. **Use the enumeration and crossing letters heavily.** With no wordplay to check, letter count is your main support.
4. **Expect a groan.** If your answer makes you smile and wince at the same time, it is probably right.

💡 **Pro tip:** in a full crossword, leave cryptic definitions until you have a few crossing letters. With two or three letters in place, the pun often jumps out.

## Why Setters Love Them, and Why They Are Rare

For setters, an &lit is the ultimate achievement: a sentence that is a perfect description and a perfect piece of wordplay at the same time. The search for &lits is a large part of the craft, and the best ones are quoted for years.

The idea is associated with the setter Ximenes, who set crosswords for The Observer and championed strict, fair clue-writing. In a strict Ximenean clue, every word must have a job, and an &lit is the extreme case: every word has two.

They are rare for a simple reason. Most phrases are not anagrams of things they describe, and most words cannot be built from their own description. When a setter finds one, it is a small piece of luck.

Cryptic definitions are rare for a different reason. Because there is no wordplay, the solver has nothing to confirm the answer. In a big crossword, crossing letters do that job. In a single daily clue with no crossings, a cryptic definition can feel unfair, which is why short-form puzzles mostly avoid them.

## Common Mistakes When Solving

Even experienced solvers trip over these clue types. Three mistakes come up again and again.

### Hunting for a definition at one end

The rule that the definition sits at the start or the end is so useful that solvers apply it automatically. In an &lit, though, the definition is the whole clue. If you have built the answer from complete wordplay and cannot find a definition, do not assume you are wrong. Read the whole sentence as a description instead.

### Dismissing the exclamation mark

It is tempting to read a closing "!" as decoration. Sometimes it is, but it is also the most common signal of an &lit. Before you ignore it, spend five seconds asking whether the clue, read plainly, describes your answer.

### Over-thinking a cryptic definition

Because cryptic definitions have no wordplay, solvers sometimes search for hidden words or anagrams that are not there. If the clue is short, ends in a question mark and has no indicator, stop looking for machinery. Find the one word with a double meaning instead.

## Writing Your Own &lit

If you enjoy setting clues, the &lit is a satisfying challenge. Most start from an anagram, and the method is simple to describe even if results are hard to find.

1. **Collect anagram pairs.** Look for words and phrases whose letters rearrange into something related. Our [anagram solver](/anagram-solver) is useful for testing candidates, and lists of famous anagrams are a good source of inspiration.
2. **Check that one side describes the other.** DIRTY ROOM and DORMITORY work because a dormitory can be a dirty room. Most anagram pairs fail this test, which is why &lits are rare.
3. **Choose a gentle indicator.** The indicator has to make sense in both readings. Words like "perhaps", "maybe" or "in a new arrangement" can soften the definition while still signalling the anagram.
4. **Read it aloud as a sentence.** If it sounds like a natural remark, you have an &lit. If it sounds like an instruction, you have a normal clue, which is fine too.

Our guide on [how to write a cryptic clue](/blog/how-to-write-a-cryptic-clue) covers the general method, and our page on [anagram clues](/cryptic-clue-types/anagram) explains the indicators that work best.

## Practice Set

Try these five clues, all written for this guide. Three are cryptic definitions and two are &lits. Answers and explanations follow below.

1. **It goes up when the rain comes down? (8)**
2. **Elegant man, in a new arrangement (1,9)**
3. **What comes before a fall? (5)**
4. **A stiff examination? (7)**
5. **Initially the eye's agitated response (4)**

### Answers

1. **UMBRELLA** — a cryptic definition. You put an umbrella up when the rain comes down.
2. **A GENTLEMAN** — an &lit. ELEGANT MAN rearranged ("in a new arrangement") gives A GENTLEMAN, and a gentleman is an elegant man.
3. **PRIDE** — a cryptic definition, from the proverb "pride comes before a fall". The surface suggests autumn or a stumble.
4. **AUTOPSY** — a cryptic definition. A "stiff" is slang for a dead body, so a stiff examination is an autopsy.
5. **TEAR** — an &lit, as explained above: the first letters spell TEAR, and the whole clue defines it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does &lit mean in a cryptic crossword?

&lit is short for "and literally so". It describes a clue in which the whole sentence is both the wordplay and the definition. In "Dirty room, perhaps (9)", DIRTY ROOM is an anagram of DORMITORY, and the whole clue also describes a dormitory.

### What does an exclamation mark mean in a cryptic clue?

An exclamation mark at the end of a cryptic clue often signals an &lit, inviting you to read the whole sentence as the definition. It is a convention, not a rule. Sometimes it simply marks a joke, and some editors mark &lits with a question mark or nothing at all.

### What is a semi-&lit clue?

A semi-&lit is a clue where the whole sentence acts as the definition, but only part of it is wordplay. In "Stressed? Turn back to these! (8)", only "stressed" reversed gives DESSERTS; the words "to these" belong to the definition alone.

### What is a cryptic definition clue?

A cryptic definition is a clue made of a single misleading description of the answer, with no wordplay. It usually ends with a question mark. "Flower of London? (6)" is a cryptic definition of THAMES, because a river is something that flows.

### What is the difference between a cryptic definition and a double definition?

A double definition gives two straight meanings of the answer, such as "Elephant's nose and a large case (5)" for TRUNK. A cryptic definition gives a single meaning phrased as a pun. If the clue splits into two honest definitions, it is a double definition; if not, it is cryptic.

### Are &lit clues common?

No. They are among the rarest clue types, because few phrases are both a description and a piece of wordplay for the same answer. In our library of 1,004 daily clues, every clue uses one of the eight standard devices. Larger puzzles might include one &lit occasionally.
