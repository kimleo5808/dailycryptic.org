Some days the Spelling Bee hands you seventy words. Other days you stare at seven letters and find a dozen. Is that just luck, or does the game follow patterns you can use?

We went through our archive of **116 NYT Spelling Bee puzzles**, from 5 July to 28 October 2026, and counted everything: every answer, every pangram, every letter and every point. The result is a picture of what a typical Spelling Bee looks like, and a few findings that change how you should play.

**What is the NYT Spelling Bee?** The Spelling Bee is a daily New York Times word puzzle with seven letters arranged in a honeycomb. You make words of at least four letters using those letters, and every word must include the centre letter. Letters can be reused. A word that uses all seven letters is called a pangram.

### How scoring works

- A four-letter word scores **1 point**.
- Longer words score **1 point per letter**, so a six-letter word scores 6.
- A pangram earns a **7-point bonus** on top.
- **Genius** is reached at 70% of the puzzle's total points; **Queen Bee** means finding every word.

<Callout>
**Key findings from 116 puzzles**

- A puzzle had **44.8 answers** on average, but anywhere from **17 to 79**.
- **Four-letter words** were 34% of all answers but only **7% of the points**.
- **64%** of puzzles had exactly one pangram; about a quarter of all pangrams were "perfect" seven-letter words.
- The letter **S never appeared** in a single hive. **I** appeared in two hives in three.
- Total points averaged **210**, so Genius typically needed about **147**.
</Callout>

## How We Did This

Our archive stores each puzzle's centre letter, six outer letters, full answer list and pangrams. We scored every answer with the official rules above and counted letters, lengths and repeats across all 116 puzzles.

The sample covers just under four months, so treat the findings as a snapshot of recent puzzles rather than the game's whole history. A different period could shift individual numbers by a few points, though the broad patterns, such as the absence of S, are built into the game. dailycryptic.org is an independent puzzle site and is not affiliated with The New York Times.

## Finding 1: The Number of Words Varies Enormously

The average puzzle had 44.8 answers and the median was 44, but those averages hide a very wide spread.

![Bar chart of answers per NYT Spelling Bee puzzle across 116 puzzles: 22 puzzles had fewer than 30 answers, 23 had 30 to 39, 24 had 40 to 49, 24 had 50 to 59 and 23 had 60 or more](/images/blog/spelling-bee-statistics/answers-per-puzzle.webp)

| Answers per puzzle | Puzzles |
|---|---|
| Fewer than 30 | 22 |
| 30–39 | 23 |
| 40–49 | 24 |
| 50–59 | 24 |
| 60 or more | 23 |

The spread is almost perfectly even. A puzzle is as likely to have fewer than 30 answers as more than 60. The smallest puzzle in our sample, on 5 August 2026, had just **17 answers** (centre Y, with A, F, H, K, O and R). The largest, on 20 September 2026, had **79** (centre H, with A, C, E, I, L and T).

**What this means for you:** do not judge your performance by word count alone. Finding 25 words is a poor day on a 79-word puzzle and an excellent one on a 30-word puzzle. The rank bar, which is based on points, is the fairer measure.

## Finding 2: Where the Points Really Come From

This is the most useful finding for anyone chasing Genius.

![Chart comparing share of words and share of points by word length across 116 Spelling Bee puzzles: four-letter words are 34% of answers but 7% of points, five-letter words 23% of answers and 24% of points, six-letter words 21% and 27%, seven-letter words 12% and 20%, eight letters or longer 10% and 22%](/images/blog/spelling-bee-statistics/points-by-length.webp)

| Word length | Share of answers | Share of points |
|---|---|---|
| 4 letters | 34% | 7% |
| 5 letters | 23% | 24% |
| 6 letters | 21% | 27% |
| 7 letters | 12% | 20% |
| 8 or more | 10% | 22% |

Four-letter words are the easiest to find and make up a third of all answers, but at one point each they contribute only about **7% of the total score**. Five- and six-letter words, by contrast, are 44% of the answers and more than half of the points.

**What this means for you:** if you are stuck below Genius, stop hunting for more four-letter words. One six-letter word is worth as much as six of them. Spend your time on longer stems and endings.

## Finding 3: Points and the Genius Threshold

The total available points varied as much as the word count. The average puzzle was worth **210 points**, with a median of 207. The leanest puzzle, on 9 September 2026, was worth only 60; the richest, on 27 September 2026, was worth 431.

Because Genius is set at 70% of the total, the Genius threshold moves with it:

| Puzzle | Total points | Genius at about |
|---|---|---|
| Leanest (9 Sep 2026) | 60 | 42 |
| Average | 210 | 147 |
| Richest (27 Sep 2026) | 431 | 302 |

On a typical day, you need around **147 points** for Genius. That might be twenty-odd mid-length words plus a pangram, but on a rich puzzle it can take twice that.

📝 **Note:** the game rounds its rank thresholds, so the exact Genius score shown in the app can differ by a point from 70% of the total.

## Finding 4: Pangrams

Every puzzle has at least one pangram, and most have only one.

| Pangrams in the puzzle | Puzzles | Share |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 74 | 64% |
| 2 | 32 | 28% |
| 3 | 7 | 6% |
| 4 | 3 | 3% |

Across the 116 puzzles there were **171 pangrams**, of which **44** were "perfect" pangrams: words that use each of the seven letters exactly once. That is about one pangram in four.

Recent perfect pangrams include NIGHTLY (26 October 2026) and RAPIDLY (21 October 2026). At the other extreme, three puzzles had four pangrams each. On 19 September 2026 the hive B, G, I, L, N, O, T produced BILTONG, BLOTTING, BOLTING and BOTTLING. On 9 October 2026 the hive Y, E, G, I, L, N, T produced GENTILITY, INTELLIGENTLY, NEGLIGENTLY and TELLINGLY. Multi-pangram puzzles usually share a common stem, so once you find one pangram it is worth rearranging it to look for more.

Pangrams are worth a lot individually, at least 14 points each including the bonus, but together they made up only about **11% of all points**. They matter, but you can reach Genius without one on most days.

💡 **Pro tip:** when hunting for the pangram, try common endings first. In our sample, 7% of all answers ended in -ING and 8% in -ED. If the hive contains I, N and G, or E and D, a pangram often ends that way.

Some letters appear in the honeycomb far more often than others.

**S never appears.** The game deliberately leaves it out, which removes easy plurals and forces you to find genuinely different words. It is the single most important rule to remember when you scan for possibilities.

Most hives had two or three vowels: 59 puzzles had two and 52 had three. Only four puzzles had a single vowel, and one had four. The centre letter was a vowel in 28 puzzles, about a quarter. The most common centre letters were **M** (11 times), **T** and **I** (10 each) and **N** (9).

## Finding 6: The Spelling Bee Regulars

Across 116 puzzles there were 5,201 answers but only 3,299 different words. Most words appeared once, but a set of regulars kept coming back.

| Word | Appearances |
|---|---|
| TINT | 11 |
| NAAN | 11 |
| NANA | 11 |
| MINI | 10 |
| MINIM | 10 |
| ANTI, ATTAIN, LILT, TAINT, TANNIN, TILL, TILT, TITAN, MITT | 9 each |

The pattern is obvious once you see it: the regulars are built from the most common hive letters, I, N, T, A and L. Whenever those letters appear together, words like TINT, TAINT and TITAN are almost certainly on the list.

**What this means for you:** when your hive contains I, N and T, run through the regulars before anything else. They are quick points.

Repeats are rarer than they look, though. Of the 3,299 different words, 2,241, about two thirds, appeared only once in our sample. The regulars are worth memorising, but most of each day's list is new, which is what keeps the puzzle fresh.

## Can You Tell a Big Puzzle From the Hive?

Before you type a single word, the seven letters already hint at how big the puzzle will be. We compared the average number of answers for hives with and without particular features.

| Hive feature | Puzzles | Average answers |
|---|---|---|
| Contains both E and D | 23 | 51.3 |
| Contains E | 53 | 47.2 |
| Three vowels | 52 | 47.5 |
| Vowel in the centre | 28 | 47.1 |
| **All puzzles** | **116** | **44.8** |
| Two vowels | 59 | 43.7 |
| Contains J, Q, X or Z | 13 | 40.8 |
| Contains I, N and G | 19 | 41.4 |

The strongest signal is **E plus D**. Hives with both letters averaged 51 answers, because so many English words end in -ED. Three vowels and an E also point to a bigger puzzle. Rare letters such as J, Q, X and Z point to a smaller one.

One result surprised us. Hives containing I, N and G, which allow the -ING ending, averaged slightly *fewer* answers than the rest. A likely explanation is that hives built around -ING tend to have fewer other vowels, which limits the shorter words. With only 19 such puzzles, though, treat that as a tendency rather than a rule.

**What this means for you:** if your hive has E and D, expect a long list and settle in. If it has a J or a Q, expect a shorter list and keep an eye on the rank bar, because Genius may come sooner than you think.

## Five Common Spelling Bee Mistakes

### 1. Forgetting that letters repeat

Every letter can be used as many times as you like. Many of the longest answers, such as INTELLIGENTLY, depend on repeating letters. If you only try words that use each letter once, you will miss a large share of the list.

### 2. Overlooking the centre letter

Every word must contain the centre letter. It is easy to find a lovely word that leaves it out. Before you type, check the centre letter is in the word.

### 3. Trying plurals

There is no S, so there are no plurals ending in S. Time spent thinking of plural forms is wasted.

### 4. Chasing four-letter words for too long

They are a third of the answers but only 7% of the points. After a first sweep, move on to longer words.

### 5. Judging the day by word count

Puzzles range from 17 to 79 answers. Use the rank bar, which is based on points, to measure how you are doing.

## The Longest Words

The longest answers in our sample were up to 13 letters:

- **INTELLIGENTLY** (13 letters, 9 October 2026)
- **GALLIVANTING**, **MINIMIZATION**, **INTIMIDATING**, **NONCONDUCTOR** and **BACCHANALIAN** (12 letters each)

Long words like these usually reuse letters heavily. INTELLIGENTLY uses only seven distinct letters across thirteen positions. That is the key to finding them: think of words that repeat letters, not words that use each letter once.

## A Data-Backed Strategy for Reaching Genius

The numbers point to a clear order of play.

### 1. Sweep the four-letter words quickly

They are a third of the answers, so they are easy confidence-builders. But give them only a few minutes, because they are worth just 7% of the points.

### 2. Hunt the pangram with common endings

Try -ING and -ED first if the hive allows them, then -LY and -ION. A pangram is worth at least 14 points and often unlocks related words.

### 3. Build families from stems

Once you find a five- or six-letter word, try adding and removing letters: TINT, TINTED, TINTING. Families of mid-length words are where most of the points live.

### 4. Look for repeated letters

The longest answers reuse letters. If the hive has a double-friendly letter like L, N or T, try words with doubled consonants.

### 5. Check the rank bar, not the word count

Because puzzles vary from 17 to 79 words, the rank bar is your only fair measure. On a lean puzzle, Genius can arrive with surprisingly few words.

## How the Spelling Bee Compares With Other Daily Word Games

The Spelling Bee rewards a different skill from its New York Times stablemates, which is why many solvers play several of them.

- **Spelling Bee** rewards **breadth of vocabulary**. There is no single answer; the more words you know, the higher you climb.
- **Wordle** rewards **deduction**. One five-letter answer, six guesses, and every guess narrows the possibilities. Our [Wordle answer page](/wordle-answer-today) explains each day's word.
- **Letter Boxed** is the Bee's closest cousin: you also build words from a fixed set of letters, but you chain them together to use every letter. See [today's Letter Boxed answers](/letter-boxed-answers-today).
- **Connections** rewards **lateral thinking**. Our study found that 77% of its hardest groups rely on wordplay rather than meaning.
- **Strands** rewards **theme spotting** and spatial search through a grid.

If you enjoy the Bee's word families and stems, you will probably enjoy cryptic crosswords too. Building SEASON from SEA and SON uses the same instinct as building TINTED from TINT.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many words are in a typical Spelling Bee?

In our analysis of 116 puzzles, the average was 44.8 words and the median 44. The range was very wide, from 17 to 79 words, and puzzles were spread almost evenly across that range, so there is no single typical size.

### How many points do you need for Genius in Spelling Bee?

Genius is 70% of the puzzle's total points. In our sample, totals averaged 210 points, so Genius typically needed about 147. On the leanest puzzle, worth 60 points, Genius came at about 42; on the richest, worth 431, it took about 302.

### Why is there never an S in Spelling Bee?

The NYT Spelling Bee deliberately excludes the letter S, which prevents simple plurals and keeps the word list more varied. In all 116 puzzles we analysed, S did not appear in a single hive.

### How many pangrams does a Spelling Bee have?

Every puzzle has at least one. In our sample, 64% had exactly one, 28% had two, and a handful had three or four. About one pangram in four was a "perfect" pangram that uses each of the seven letters exactly once.

### What are the most common Spelling Bee letters?

In our sample of 116 hives, the most frequent letters were I (66% of hives), N (60%), A (58%), L (51%), O (50%) and T (49%). The rarest were J, Q and Z, and S never appeared.

### What is the best strategy for Spelling Bee?

Sweep the short words quickly, then focus on five- and six-letter words, which supply more than half the points. Hunt the pangram using common endings such as -ING and -ED, build word families from each stem, and judge your progress by the rank bar rather than your word count.
