Every cryptic clue you solve carries a century of history. The idea that a clue should contain a definition *and* a piece of wordplay, the rule that every word must earn its place, even the habit of signing puzzles with a pseudonym: none of these were inevitable. They were invented, argued over and refined by a handful of setters, mostly in British newspapers, over roughly a hundred years.

This is the short version of that story, from a single diamond-shaped puzzle in New York to the one-clue daily cryptics on today's phones.

**What is a cryptic crossword?** A cryptic crossword is a crossword in which every clue works twice: once as a plain definition of the answer, and once as wordplay that builds the same answer another way, through anagrams, hidden words, sound-alikes and similar devices. The style developed in Britain in the 1920s and 1930s.

## 1913: The First Crossword

The crossword began in the United States. On 21 December 1913, the New York World published a puzzle by Arthur Wynne, a journalist from Liverpool, in its Sunday supplement. It was diamond-shaped, used no black squares at all, and was called a "word-cross". Soon afterwards the name was reversed to "cross-word", and it stuck.

Wynne's clues were plain definitions. There was no wordplay at all: a clue simply described the answer. That style, refined over the following decades, is still the basis of the American crossword today.

The puzzle caught on slowly at first and then explosively. In 1924 a new publishing company, Simon & Schuster, released a book of crossword puzzles that became a bestseller, and a full crossword craze swept the United States.

## 1924–1930: The Crossword Crosses the Atlantic

The craze reached Britain in 1924, when the **Sunday Express** began publishing crosswords, starting with puzzles by Wynne. Other papers followed: the **Daily Telegraph** from 1925, **The Manchester Guardian** from 1929 and **The Times** from 1930.

These early British puzzles were not cryptic in the modern sense. Their clues were mostly definitions, sometimes with a playful twist. But British setters and solvers quickly began to push the form. Plain definitions were too easy for a readership raised on word games, riddles and acrostics, and setters started to hide their answers in more devious ways.

## 1926: Torquemada and the Birth of the Cryptic

The setter who did most to turn the crossword cryptic was **Edward Powys Mathers**, a poet and translator who signed his puzzles **Torquemada**, after the notorious Spanish Inquisitor. He set for The Saturday Westminster from 1925 and began at **The Observer** on 14 March 1926, where he set around 670 puzzles until his death in 1939.

Torquemada is generally credited as the first setter to use cryptic clues exclusively. His clues were witty, allusive and often fiendish. He also used unusual grids and themes, and his puzzles were hard enough that solving one became a mark of distinction.

What his clues lacked was consistency. Torquemada played by his own rules, and a clue might be a pun, a riddle or a piece of wordplay with no clear signal of which. The next great Observer setter would fix that.

## 1930: The Times and The Listener

In 1930 two very different crosswords began. **The Times** launched its daily puzzle, which would become the most famous cryptic crossword in the world and, eventually, the home of a national solving championship.

The same year, **The Listener**, the BBC's weekly magazine, began a crossword series aimed at expert solvers. Listener puzzles used barred grids, elaborate themes and extra rules, and they became the proving ground for some of the most ambitious setters of the century. The series outlived the magazine itself.

## 1939: Ximenes and the Rules of Fair Play

When Torquemada died in 1939, his place at The Observer was taken by **Derrick Somerset Macnutt**, a schoolmaster who chose the pseudonym **Ximenes**, after another Grand Inquisitor.

Ximenes kept the wit but added discipline. He insisted that every clue should contain a precise definition, fair wordplay and nothing else. Every anagram needed an indicator; every abbreviation needed to be in a standard dictionary; every word needed a job. In 1966 he set out his principles in *Ximenes on the Art of the Crossword*, which remains the foundational text of clue-writing.

![The Observer's line of cryptic setters: Torquemada (Edward Powys Mathers) from 1926 to 1939, Ximenes (Derrick Somerset Macnutt) from 1939 to 1971, and Azed (Jonathan Crowther) from 1972](/images/blog/history-of-cryptic-crosswords/observer-setters.webp)

His influence is so strong that "Ximenean" is now the standard word for a clue that follows the rules. Most daily cryptics today are broadly Ximenean, with occasional flourishes from the looser, "libertarian" tradition.

Another setter of the period, **Afrit** (Alistair Ferguson Ritchie), summed up the ethic in a line still quoted by every serious setter. In his 1949 book *Armchair Crosswords*, he wrote: "You need not mean what you say, but you must say what you mean." The surface of a clue may mislead you, but its cryptic reading must be literally true. Our guide on [how to write a cryptic clue](/blog/how-to-write-a-cryptic-clue) shows how those rules work in practice.

## 1942–1944: Crosswords Go to War

The Second World War produced the two most famous crossword stories ever told, both involving The Daily Telegraph.

### The Bletchley Park contest

In 1942 the Telegraph held a crossword-solving competition. Afterwards, some of the fastest solvers were contacted by the War Office and invited to work on codebreaking at **Bletchley Park**, where Allied cryptanalysts were attacking German ciphers. Accounts say the qualifying target was to finish the puzzle in under twelve minutes. Whatever the exact details, the story captures something real: the mental habits of a cryptic solver, spotting hidden patterns and testing interpretations, are close cousins of codebreaking.

### The D-Day codeword scare

In the weeks before the D-Day landings of June 1944, codewords connected to the invasion began appearing as answers in the Telegraph crossword. On 2 May 1944, UTAH, the codename of one of the landing beaches, appeared as an answer. It was followed by OMAHA, another beach, and later OVERLORD, MULBERRY and NEPTUNE, the names of the operation and its key elements.

MI5 investigated. The setter turned out to be **Leonard Dawe**, the headmaster of a school who had been compiling the paper's crosswords for years. He was questioned and eventually cleared. Decades later, one of his former pupils said that Dawe used to ask boys to suggest words for his grids, and that some had picked up the codewords from soldiers stationed nearby. Whether that explains every coincidence remains a matter of debate, but the episode became one of the great puzzle legends of the war.

## The Post-War Golden Age

After the war, the cryptic crossword became a fixture of British life.

At **The Guardian**, **Araucaria** (the Reverend John Graham), who set for the paper from the late 1950s until his death in 2013, became the most beloved setter of his generation. He was the champion of the libertarian style, bending Ximenean rules when a joke or a brilliant surface demanded it. In one of his last puzzles he used the crossword itself to tell solvers he had cancer, a measure of how personal the bond between setter and solver can be.

In 1970 The Times launched its **Crossword Championship**, turning solving into a competitive sport, with finalists racing to complete puzzles against the clock.

At The Observer, Ximenes died in 1971, and in March 1972 the puzzle passed to **Jonathan Crowther**, who took the name **Azed**, Deza reversed, after yet another Inquisitor. The Azed puzzle has appeared under his name ever since, keeping the Ximenean tradition alive for more than half a century.

Crosswords reached popular culture, too. The novelist **Colin Dexter**, a keen crossword solver, named his detective **Inspector Morse** after Sir Jeremy Morse, a banker and fellow crossword enthusiast, and filled the books with crossword references.

## The Setters Behind the Pseudonyms

Cryptic setters have signed their work with pseudonyms almost from the start, and the names themselves are part of the game. Torquemada, Ximenes and Azed were all named after Spanish Inquisitors, a joke about the torment their puzzles inflicted on solvers.

| Pseudonym | Real name | Best known for | Active |
|---|---|---|---|
| Torquemada | Edward Powys Mathers | The Observer | 1926–1939 |

| Afrit | Alistair Ferguson Ritchie | The Listener; *Armchair Crosswords* (1949) | Mid-20th century |

A pseudonym does more than hide an identity. It lets regular solvers recognise a setter's style before they read a single clue, and many solvers have favourite setters whose puzzles they seek out. The Times is a notable exception: its puzzles are unsigned.

## Cryptics Around the World

The cryptic style spread wherever British newspapers had influence. Today, daily cryptic crosswords are a staple in **Ireland**, **Australia**, **New Zealand**, **South Africa** and **India**, and they are popular in **Canada**. Each country has its own setters, its own house styles and its own local references.

The United States went a different way. American newspapers kept the plain-definition style of Wynne's first puzzle, and the New York Times, which began publishing crosswords in 1942, became its most famous home. Cryptics have a devoted American following, though, and magazines such as The Nation have run cryptic crosswords for decades. Our comparison of [cryptic and regular crosswords](/blog/cryptic-vs-regular-crossword) explains how the two traditions differ.

## The Digital Age

The internet changed how people solve. Newspapers put their puzzles online, apps made it possible to solve on a phone, and solving blogs began publishing detailed explanations of every clue in major puzzles. For the first time, a beginner could see exactly how an experienced solver reasoned through a hard clue.

That was a bigger change than it sounds. For most of the twentieth century, a newspaper printed the answers to a cryptic the next day, or a week later for prize puzzles, but rarely explained them. If you could not see why an answer was right, you had to work it out yourself or ask a more experienced solver. Explanations turned cryptic solving from a skill passed between friends and family into one anyone could teach themselves.

The other change was format. NYT games such as **Wordle** and **Connections** showed that millions of people would happily play one short word puzzle a day. Connections in particular brought cryptic-style wordplay to a mass audience: our [analysis of 660 Connections puzzles](/blog/nyt-connections-purple-category) found that 77% of its hardest groups rely on wordplay.

## 2024: One Clue a Day

In 2024, **Minute Cryptic** took the daily-puzzle idea and applied it to cryptics. Founded by **Angas Tiernan** in Melbourne, it began with short daily videos on social media explaining a single cryptic clue, then grew into a website and an app offering one clue a day with hints and explanations.

The format solved the oldest problem in cryptic crosswords: getting started. A full grid can be intimidating; one clue is not. It also turned the explanation, once hidden in the back of a newspaper the next day, into part of the experience. Single-clue cryptics, including our own [daily minute cryptic](/minute-cryptic-today), have since become one of the easiest ways into the hobby. dailycryptic.org is independent and is not affiliated with Minute Cryptic.

## What a Century of History Means for You

Almost every convention you meet in a modern cryptic has a story behind it:

- **Indicators** exist because Ximenes insisted that wordplay must be signalled.
- **Abbreviations** must be in a standard dictionary because fairness demanded it. Our article on [why love is O](/blog/cryptic-crossword-abbreviations) explains where many of them come from.
- **The definition at one end** is a Ximenean rule that makes clues solvable.
- **Pseudonyms** are a habit inherited from Torquemada, Ximenes and Azed.
- **&lit clues**, where the whole clue is both definition and wordplay, are the ultimate expression of Ximenean economy. See our guide to [&lit clues](/blog/and-lit-clues).

Knowing the history makes the rules feel less arbitrary. They are the accumulated wisdom of a century of setters trying to make puzzles that are hard but fair.

If you are new to cryptics, you are joining a tradition that has always made room for beginners. Every expert solver once stared at a first clue and saw nothing but nonsense. The difference today is that you can read the explanation the same day, and learn from every clue instead of piecing the rules together alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When was the first cryptic crossword?

There is no single first cryptic crossword, because the style evolved gradually. The first crossword of any kind appeared in the New York World on 21 December 1913. Edward Powys Mathers, writing as Torquemada, is generally credited as the first setter to use cryptic clues exclusively, at The Observer from 1926.

### Who invented the cryptic crossword?

No one person invented it, but Torquemada (Edward Powys Mathers) did most to create the form in the 1920s, and Ximenes (Derrick Somerset Macnutt) gave it its rules from 1939 onwards. His 1966 book, *Ximenes on the Art of the Crossword*, defined the conventions most cryptics still follow.

### Why are cryptic crosswords British?

The crossword arrived in Britain in 1924, and British setters quickly moved from plain definitions to wordplay, drawing on a long tradition of riddles and word games. American papers kept the plain-definition style of the original 1913 puzzle, so the two traditions developed separately.

### Were crosswords really used to recruit codebreakers?

Yes, in a fashion. In 1942 a Daily Telegraph crossword-solving competition was followed by approaches from the War Office to some of the fastest solvers, inviting them to work at Bletchley Park. The often-quoted target of finishing in under twelve minutes comes from later accounts.

### What happened with the D-Day crossword?

In May and June 1944, codewords linked to the D-Day landings, including UTAH, OMAHA and OVERLORD, appeared as answers in the Daily Telegraph crossword. MI5 questioned the setter, headmaster Leonard Dawe, and cleared him. A former pupil later said boys had suggested the words after hearing them from soldiers.

### Who created Minute Cryptic?

Minute Cryptic was founded by Angas Tiernan, who is based in Melbourne. It began in 2024 with daily videos explaining a single cryptic clue and grew into a website and app offering one clue a day. dailycryptic.org is a separate, independent site with its own daily clue.
