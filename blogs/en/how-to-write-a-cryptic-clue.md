Writing a cryptic clue looks like a dark art. In practice, it is a craft with a short list of rules, and most bad clues break the same few.

We know because we have just been through it. In October 2026 we reviewed every clue waiting in our daily queue, **747 clues** scheduled from 30 October 2026 to 14 November 2028, and changed the wording of **323 of them**. We also went back and repaired **30 clues** that had already been published. This guide is what that work taught us.

**What is a fair cryptic clue?** A fair cryptic clue contains a definition of the answer at its start or end, plus wordplay that produces exactly the same answer by a second route. Every word in the clue has a job, and the clue must literally say what it means, even when its surface story misleads you.

## The Three Rules of a Fair Clue

Before the method, the rules. Almost every flaw in the second half of this article breaks one of them.

### Rule 1: One definition, at the start or the end

A cryptic clue has exactly one definition, and it sits at one end of the clue. It must match the answer's part of speech. If the answer is a noun, the definition is a noun phrase; if it is a verb, the definition is a verb.

### Rule 2: Wordplay that produces exactly the answer

The wordplay must build the answer letter for letter, using a recognised device: anagram, charade, container, deletion, hidden word, reversal, homophone or a second definition. Close is not good enough. If the wordplay produces a word one letter off the answer, the clue is broken.

### Rule 3: Every word earns its place

Each word is part of the definition, part of the wordplay, or a short link word between them. Words that are only there to make the surface sound nicer are called padding, and fair clues avoid them.

These rules descend from the setter Afrit's famous injunction: **"You need not mean what you say, but you must say what you mean."** The surface may tell a misleading story. The cryptic reading must still be literally true. Our guide to [cryptic crossword jargon](/blog/cryptic-crossword-glossary) explains the terms used throughout, with real clues.

## Step 1: Pick an Answer and Take It Apart

Start with the answer, not the clue. Write it in capitals and look at it from every angle before you write a single word of clue.

Ask four questions:

1. **Does it split into smaller words?** SEASON is SEA + SON. CARPET is CAR + PET. Splits give you charades.
2. **Is it an anagram of something useful?** SILENT is LISTEN. DESSERTS reversed is STRESSED. Rearrangements and reversals give you some of the most satisfying clues.
3. **Does it hide inside a natural phrase?** SEASON hides in "these as one". Hidden words are the easiest device to write well.
4. **Does it have two unrelated meanings?** SEASON is a time of year and a verb meaning to add flavour. Two meanings give you a double definition.

We will follow **SEASON** through every step of the method.

![The five steps of writing a cryptic clue, shown with the answer SEASON: take it apart (SEA + SON, hidden in "these as one"), choose a definition ("summer, perhaps"), choose a device (charade: salt water + a boy), write the surface, and test every word, giving "Salt water and a boy for summer, perhaps (6)"](/images/blog/how-to-write-a-cryptic-clue/five-steps.webp)

## Step 2: Choose the Definition

Make a list of every way to define the answer, then pick the one that will make the best surface.

For SEASON, the options include:

- **"Time of year"**: accurate but flat.
- **"Flavour"**: the verb sense, which is good for misdirection because "flavour" looks like a noun.
- **"Summer, perhaps"**: a definition by example, flagged by "perhaps". It is vivid and opens up a beach-themed surface.

Check the part of speech every time. If you define SEASON as "flavour" in the verb sense, the rest of the clue must read so that "flavour" can be a verb. If you define it as "summer, perhaps", it must work as a noun.

💡 **Pro tip:** definitions by example need a flag such as "perhaps", "say" or "for one". Summer is a season, but a season is not necessarily summer, so the flag is what keeps the clue fair.

## Step 3: Choose the Wordplay

Now choose the device. For SEASON we have three realistic options:

| Device | Wordplay | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Charade | SEA + SON: "salt water" + "a boy" | Natural and easy to make into a surface |
| Hidden word | inside "these as one" | Neat, but "these as one" is hard to make sound natural |
| Double definition | "time of year" + "flavour" | Clean, but very short and easy to spot |

### Choosing an indicator that reads naturally

Most devices need an indicator, and the indicator is where many surfaces fall apart. "Anagram of", "reversed" and "hidden in" are accurate but clunky. The best indicators are ordinary words that fit the story while still telling the solver what to do.

Three published clues from our archive show the idea:

- **"Players from carthorse running loose (9)"**: "running loose" is an anagram indicator, and it suits a sentence about a horse. CARTHORSE rearranged gives ORCHESTRA.
- **"Royal when lager is poured back (5)"**: "poured back" is a reversal indicator, and it suits a sentence about beer. LAGER reversed gives REGAL.
- **"Thin soup: both clutching resistance (5)"**: "clutching" is a container indicator, and it suits a sentence about holding on. BOTH around R gives BROTH.

In each case the indicator does two jobs at once: it carries the surface story and it gives the instruction. When you find an indicator that does both, the clue usually falls into place around it.

### Turning each piece into a clue word

Each piece of the wordplay needs its own clue word. "Salt water" for SEA is fine, because the sea is salt water. "A boy" for SON is a long-established crossword convention. If you need single letters, our [cryptic abbreviations reference](/cryptic-abbreviations) shows which ones solvers will recognise.

## Step 4: Write a Surface That Tells a Story

The surface is the sentence a solver reads first. It should make sense on its own, ideally telling a small story that has nothing to do with the answer.

Put the pieces in order and look for a sentence:

- Definition + wordplay: "Summer, perhaps: salt water and a boy (6)". Accurate, but it reads like a list.
- Wordplay + definition: **"Salt water and a boy for summer, perhaps (6)"**. Now it reads like a holiday memory: a beach, a child, summer.

The second version wins. "For" is a link word that sits naturally between the two halves, and the surface conjures a picture without giving the answer away.

📖 **Further reading:** our guide to [surface reading](/blog/surface-reading-cryptic-clues) looks at what makes a surface convincing from the solver's side.

## Step 5: Test It Like a Solver

Finally, read the clue as a suspicious solver would, word by word. "Salt water and a boy for summer, perhaps (6)" passes every test:

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Definition at one end? | "summer, perhaps", at the end |
| Definition matches the part of speech? | Noun for noun |
| Wordplay produces exactly the answer? | SEA + SON = SEASON |
| Every word has a job? | "and" joins the parts; "for" links to the definition |
| Answer absent from the clue? | Yes |
| Enumeration correct? | (6) |
| Any capitals or punctuation misleading unfairly? | No |

If any test fails, go back a step. Most clues need two or three rounds before they are right.

## The Nine Flaws We Fixed Most Often

When we reviewed our queue, the same problems came up again and again. Some we could count automatically from the original text; others we logged by hand as we edited. All the before-and-after examples below are clues that had already been published on this site, which is why we can show them in full.

![Bar chart of problems found in 747 queued clues: capitalised fodder 116, "from CAPS" construction 45, "X, or Y" double definitions 37, formulaic "then" charades 34; overall, 323 of 747 clues needed new wording](/images/blog/how-to-write-a-cryptic-clue/flaws.webp)

### 1. Capitalised fodder

The most common problem by far. Writing the fodder in capitals, as in "Mythical yarn from TALL + TALE (8)", tells the solver exactly which letters to use and turns the clue into a sum. It appeared in 116 of the 747 original queued clues.

> **Before:** Mythical yarn from TALL + TALE (8)
> **After:** Exaggeration: lofty account (4,4) — *puzzle of 6 June 2026*

The fix hides each piece behind a definition: "lofty" for TALL and "account" for TALE. Notice the enumeration fix too: a two-word answer should be shown as (4,4), not (8).

### 2. Not a cryptic clue at all

Twenty of our early published clues were word-association riddles rather than cryptic clues. They had no definition and no wordplay, just a list of exclamations.

> **Before:** Right click! Left click! Copy! Paste! Paste! (4)
> **After:** Record sticky strip (4) — *puzzle of 15 June 2026*

The new clue is a double definition: to TAPE is to record, and tape is a sticky strip.

### 3. Wordplay that does not produce the answer

The subtlest flaw: the clue looks right, but the wordplay builds a different word.

> **Before:** Heartless flair makes something just (4)
> **After:** Holy man, heartless, is just (4) — *puzzle of 28 July 2026*

FLAIR without its middle letter gives FLIR, not FAIR. The fix uses FAKIR, a holy man, whose middle letter K comes out cleanly to leave FAIR.

### 4. A definition that does not match the answer

> **Before:** Vegetable within the spinach arrived (4)
> **After:** Scorch a bit of the peach arrangement (4) — *puzzle of 10 July 2026*

The hidden word was right, CHAR inside "spinaCH ARrived", but char is not a vegetable. (Chard is.) The fix defines CHAR correctly as "scorch".

### 5. Homophones that do not sound alike

> **Before:** Flower that sounds like quarrels (4)
> **After:** Lines of seats, we hear, for a flower (4) — *puzzle of 19 July 2026*

"Rows" meaning quarrels rhymes with *cows*, so it does not sound like ROSE. "Rows" meaning lines of seats does. Always say a homophone out loud, in the sense the clue uses.

### 6. The answer appears in the clue

> **Before:** Weapon is sword cut short (5)
> **After:** Weapon hidden in password (5) — *puzzle of 24 July 2026*

The original spelled out the answer, and "cut short" would have removed a letter from it anyway. The fix hides SWORD inside "pas**SWORD**".

### 7. "X, or Y" double definitions

Thirty-seven of the original queued clues joined two definitions with "or", in the style of "Spy, or burrowing animal" for MOLE. It is accurate but reads like a dictionary entry. Better double definitions read as a single sentence: "Elephant's nose and a large case (5)" for TRUNK, or "Book that is new (5)" for NOVEL.

### 8. Formulaic "A, then B" charades

Thirty-four charades used "then" as glue, as in "Ranch hand: cow then boy". It works, but every clue sounds the same. A real link makes a story: "Renter: ten joined to an insect (6)" for TENANT is still a simple charade, but it reads like a sentence.

### 9. Padding and loose ends

The last group covers smaller problems we logged by hand: padding words that do no work, indicators that point the wrong way, and the same word used in both the definition and the wordplay. Each one is minor, but together they make a clue feel sloppy. The test table in Step 5 catches all of them.

| Flaw | How to catch it |
|---|---|
| Capitalised fodder | No word in the clue should be in capitals unless it is a proper noun |
| Not a cryptic clue | Can you point to the definition and the wordplay separately? |
| Wordplay misses the answer | Write the wordplay out letter by letter |
| Definition mismatch | Would a dictionary accept the definition for the answer? |
| False homophone | Say both words aloud, in the sense the clue uses |
| Answer in the clue | Search the clue for the answer before you publish |
| "X, or Y" | Read the clue aloud: does it sound like a sentence? |
| "then" charades | Try a verb or preposition that tells a story instead |
| Padding | Cover each word: does the clue still work without it? If so, delete it |

## Writing a Cryptic Clue for a Name, Birthday or Message

Many people search for how to write a cryptic clue for a birthday card, a treasure hunt or a proposal. The method is the same, with one change: make it **easier** than a newspaper clue, because the reader is not a seasoned solver.

Three tips:

1. **Use a hidden word or a simple charade.** They are the easiest devices to spot.
2. **Give the enumeration**, and consider adding a hint word in brackets.
3. **Make the definition personal.** "Our favourite boy" is a perfectly good definition for a name.

Here are three examples written for this guide:

> **Boy found at the bottom (3)** — TOM is hidden in "botTOM".

> **Girl going back and forth (4)** — ANNA reads the same in both directions.

> **Zero affection? Quite the opposite! (4)** — LOVE means zero in tennis, and love is anything but zero affection.

The last one shows how far you can push a personal message. It is a double definition with a joke attached, and the exclamation mark tells the reader to enjoy it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you write a cryptic clue?

Start with the answer and break it apart: split it, rearrange it, reverse it or hide it in a phrase. Choose a definition for one end of the clue, choose one wordplay device, and write a natural sentence that combines them. Finally, test every word. "Salt water and a boy for summer, perhaps (6)" gives SEA + SON = SEASON.

### What makes a cryptic clue unfair?

A clue is unfair when its wordplay does not produce exactly the answer, its definition is wrong, or it has words that do no job. In our review of 747 clues, the most common faults were capitalised fodder (116 clues), "X, or Y" double definitions (37) and formulaic "then" charades (34).

### What is the easiest type of cryptic clue to write?

Hidden word clues are usually the easiest to write, because the answer sits inside a phrase and you only need a definition and an indicator such as "in" or "some". Charades are a close second. Anagrams are harder than they look, because the fodder must sound natural.

### Can a cryptic clue have no indicator?

Yes. Double definitions and cryptic definitions have no indicator, because there is no operation to signal. Every other device needs one: an anagram needs a word like "mixed", a hidden word needs a word like "in", and a homophone needs a word like "we hear".

### How do I write a cryptic clue for a birthday card?

Keep it simple. Use a hidden word or a charade, include the letter count in brackets, and make the definition personal. For example, "Boy found at the bottom (3)" hides TOM in "botTOM". If the reader is new to cryptics, add a one-word hint underneath.

### How long does it take to write a good cryptic clue?

A simple clue can take five minutes; a great one can take an hour or more. In our own review, most clues needed two or three rounds of testing before every word did a job and the surface read naturally. Speed comes with practice and a reliable checklist.
