Cryptic crosswords come with their own vocabulary. Solvers talk about fodder and anagrinds, setters argue about Ximenean rules, and forums are full of people asking what an "unch" is. None of it is hard, but nobody explains it all in one place.

This article explains that jargon by showing it at work. It covers **63 terms**, grouped by what they describe: the parts of a clue, the clue types, the signal words, punctuation, the grid, and the culture of setting and solving. Each definition is short enough to read in a few seconds, and most come with a real clue from our archive. If you prefer a straight alphabetical lookup, our A–Z [cryptic crossword glossary](/cryptic-glossary) is the quickest reference.

**What is a cryptic crossword?** A cryptic crossword is a puzzle in which every clue works twice. One part of the clue is an ordinary definition of the answer. The rest is wordplay that builds the same answer another way, through anagrams, hidden words, sound-alikes and similar tricks.

**Start here if you are new:** the five terms you will meet in almost every explanation are **definition**, **wordplay**, **indicator**, **fodder** and **surface**. They are the first five entries below, and one clue shows all of them at once.
</Callout>

## The Parts of a Clue

Every cryptic clue can be taken apart into a small set of pieces. Here they are all at once, in a clue from our archive published on 31 August 2026:

> **Players from carthorse running loose (9)**
> "Players" is the definition. "from" is a link word. "carthorse" is the fodder. "running loose" is the anagram indicator. CARTHORSE rearranged gives **ORCHESTRA**.

![Anatomy of a cryptic clue: in "Players from carthorse running loose (9)", "Players" is the definition, "from" is a link word, "carthorse" is the fodder and "running loose" is the anagram indicator; rearranging CARTHORSE gives ORCHESTRA](/images/blog/cryptic-crossword-glossary/anatomy.webp)

### Definition

The **definition** is the part of a cryptic clue that means the answer, just like a clue in an ordinary crossword. It almost always sits at the very start or the very end of the clue, never in the middle. In "Players from carthorse running loose (9)", the definition is "Players".

### Wordplay

**Wordplay** is the rest of the clue: the instructions for building the answer a second way. It might rearrange letters, hide the answer inside a phrase, join small words together or play on sound. Solving a cryptic clue means finding where the definition ends and the wordplay begins.

### Indicator

An **indicator** is a word or phrase that tells you which kind of wordplay to use. "Running loose" signals an anagram. "Sent back" signals a reversal. "We hear" signals a homophone. Learning indicators is the fastest way to improve as a solver.

### Fodder

**Fodder** is the raw material the wordplay works on: the letters you rearrange, reverse or search inside. In an anagram clue it is the word or words to be jumbled, such as "carthorse". In a hidden-word clue it is the phrase that contains the answer.

### Surface reading

The **surface reading**, or **surface**, is what the clue appears to say when you read it as a normal sentence. A good surface tells a convincing little story that has nothing to do with the answer. "Players from carthorse running loose" pictures a horse bolting, not an orchestra. Our guide to [surface reading](/blog/surface-reading-cryptic-clues) goes deeper.

### Link word

A **link word** joins the definition to the wordplay, such as "from", "for", "when", "is" or "gives". It reads naturally in the surface but does no work in the wordplay. In "Royal when lager is poured back (5)", "when" is the link: LAGER poured back gives **REGAL**.

### Enumeration

The **enumeration** is the number or numbers in brackets at the end of a clue, telling you the length of the answer. "(9)" means one nine-letter word. "(4,4)" means two four-letter words, and "(5-3)" means a hyphenated answer.

### Misdirection

**Misdirection** is the art of making a clue's surface point you the wrong way. Setters choose words with two meanings and use the more obvious one in the surface. "Flower" in a clue might be a bloom, or it might be a river, something that flows.

### Lift and separate

**Lift and separate** is a technique where the setter writes a phrase that must be split apart to solve the clue. A clue that mentions "butterfly" may need you to read it as "butter" and "fly" separately. It is one of the harder skills to learn because the surface hides the split completely.

### Definition by example

A **definition by example**, often shortened to **DBE**, defines the answer through one example of it. It is usually flagged by a word like "perhaps", "say" or "for example". In "Thames, perhaps, seen in drivers (5)", published on 30 June 2026, the Thames is an example of a **RIVER**, hidden in "dRIVERs".

### Abbreviation

An **abbreviation** is a conventional letter or short string that a clue word stands for, such as O for "love" or "nothing". In "Moan: gran holding nothing (5)", "nothing" gives O, and GRAN holding O gives **GROAN**. Our A–Z [abbreviations reference](/cryptic-abbreviations) lists them, and our article on [why love is O](/blog/cryptic-crossword-abbreviations) explains where they come from.

## Clue Types

Most cryptic clues use one of a small number of devices. Our [guide to cryptic clue types](/cryptic-clue-types) covers each in depth; these are the short definitions.

### Anagram

An **anagram** clue asks you to rearrange the letters of the fodder to make the answer. An indicator such as "mixed", "broken" or "reorganised" tells you to shuffle. In "Christmas decoration when enlist is reorganised (6)", ENLIST rearranged gives **TINSEL**.

### Charade

A **charade** builds the answer by joining smaller pieces in order, like a word sum. In "Renter: ten joined to an insect (6)", TEN + ANT gives **TENANT**. Charades often use abbreviations to supply single letters.

### Container

A **container** clue puts one piece of the answer inside another. Indicators include "holding", "clutching", "eating" and "around". In "Thin soup: both clutching resistance (5)", BOTH holding R (resistance) gives **BROTH**. Some solvers call these **insertion** or **envelope** clues.

### Deletion

A **deletion** clue removes one or more letters from a word to leave the answer. The indicator tells you which letters to remove: the first, the last, the middle, or a specific letter. Three common kinds have names of their own: beheadment, curtailment and the heartless clue.

![Three ways to lose a letter in a deletion clue: beheadment removes the first letter (WHEAT becomes HEAT), curtailment removes the last letter (BRAND becomes BRAN), and a heartless clue removes the middle letter (FAKIR becomes FAIR)](/images/blog/cryptic-crossword-glossary/deletions.webp)

### Beheadment

A **beheadment** removes the first letter of a word. Indicators include "headless", "losing its head" and "not starting". In "Warmth from wheat losing its head (4)", WHEAT minus W gives **HEAT**.

### Curtailment

A **curtailment** removes the last letter. Indicators include "almost", "endless", "nearly" and "cut short". In "Breakfast fibre: label, almost (4)", BRAND (label) minus its last letter gives **BRAN**.

### Heartless

A **heartless** clue removes the middle letter or letters. In "Holy man, heartless, is just (4)", FAKIR (a holy man) minus its middle K gives **FAIR**.

### Hidden word

A **hidden word** clue conceals the answer inside a longer word or across several words, in the correct order. Indicators include "hidden in", "some" and "part of". In "Waterway hidden in African Alps (5)", the answer is inside afri**CAN AL**ps: **CANAL**. Solvers sometimes call these **lurkers**.

### Reversal

A **reversal** clue writes a word backwards to give the answer. Indicators include "sent back", "turned around" and "reflected". In "Sweet courses when stressed is turned around (8)", STRESSED reversed gives **DESSERTS**.

### Homophone

A **homophone** clue gives you a word that sounds like the answer but is spelled differently. Indicators include "we hear", "reportedly" and "sounds like". In "Hidden store that sounds like money (5)", **CACHE** sounds like CASH. See our guide to [homophone clues](/blog/how-to-solve-homophone-clues).

### Double definition

A **double definition** gives two different meanings of the same answer, side by side, with no wordplay. In "Elephant's nose and a large case (5)", both halves define **TRUNK**.

### Cryptic definition

A **cryptic definition** is a single, misleading description of the answer, with no separate wordplay. It often ends with a question mark. "Flower of London? (6)" is a cryptic definition of the **THAMES**, a river being something that flows. *(Example written for this glossary.)*

### &lit

An **&lit** clue (short for "and literally so") is one where the entire clue is both the wordplay and the definition. Every word works twice. In "Dirty room, perhaps (9)", DIRTY ROOM is an anagram of **DORMITORY**, and the whole clue also describes one. *(Example written for this glossary.)* They are rare, often marked with an exclamation mark, and much admired.

### Semi-&lit

A **semi-&lit** is a clue where the whole clue is the definition, but only part of it is wordplay. It sits between an ordinary clue and a full &lit. Some solvers use the term loosely for any clue whose wordplay and definition overlap.

### Spoonerism

A **spoonerism** clue swaps the opening sounds of two words, named after the Reverend William Spooner, who was famous for such slips. "Spooner's prying chef finds a comfortable corner (4,4)" turns NOSY COOK into **COSY NOOK**. *(Example written for this glossary.)*

### Letter selection

A **letter selection** clue takes specific letters from the fodder: odd or even letters, or the first or last letters of several words. In "Foot digit found oddly in those (3)", the odd letters of T-h-O-s-E give **TOE**. *(Example written for this glossary.)*

### Acrostic

An **acrostic** clue builds the answer from the first letters of a run of words, signalled by "initially", "at first" or "leaders". In "Listening organ: every animal responds initially (3)", the initials of Every Animal Responds give **EAR**. *(Example written for this glossary.)*

### Compound anagram

A **compound anagram** is one where the answer and another word, taken together, are an anagram of the fodder. You have to identify the extra word and remove its letters before the answer appears. They are among the hardest devices and appear mostly in advanced puzzles.

### Subtractive anagram

A **subtractive anagram** asks you to remove some letters from the fodder and rearrange what is left. The clue tells you both what to remove and that the rest is mixed, which makes these long and demanding to read.

### Rebus

A **rebus** represents a word with pictures, symbols or letter positions, such as "MAN BOARD" for "man overboard". Rebus thinking turns up in some cryptic clues, especially in short-form daily puzzles. We explore the link in [rebus puzzles and cryptic clues](/blog/rebus-puzzles-and-cryptic-clues).

## Signal Words

Indicators come in families, one for each kind of wordplay. Our [list of cryptic crossword indicators](/cryptic-indicators) has hundreds of examples; these are the names solvers use for them.

### Anagrind

**Anagrind** is solvers' slang for an anagram indicator, a blend of "anagram" and "indicator". Common anagrinds suggest movement, damage or change: "broken", "wild", "mixed", "cooked", "upset" and "reorganised".

### Container indicator

A **container indicator** tells you to put one part inside another. Look for words about holding, wrapping, eating or surrounding: "holding", "clutching", "keeps", "around", "about" and "outside".

### Insertion indicator

An **insertion indicator** is the mirror image of a container indicator: it tells you to put one part *into* another. Typical examples are "in", "inside", "entering" and "going into".

### Reversal indicator

A **reversal indicator** tells you to write a word backwards. In across clues, it often suggests going back or west: "back", "returning", "going west". In down clues, it suggests rising: "up", "rising", "sent up".

### Homophone indicator

A **homophone indicator** tells you to listen rather than look: "we hear", "reportedly", "they say", "on the radio", "sounds like" and "by the sound of it".

### Hidden word indicator

A **hidden word indicator** tells you the answer is concealed in the clue: "hidden in", "found in", "part of", "some" and "in". The shorter the indicator, the easier it is to miss.

### Deletion indicator

A **deletion indicator** tells you to remove letters, and usually which ones: "headless" (first letter), "endless" or "almost" (last letter), "heartless" (middle) or "without" plus a specific letter.

### Selection indicator

A **selection indicator** tells you to pick particular letters: "initially" or "at first" (first letters), "finally" (last letters), "oddly" (odd letters), "evenly" (even letters) or "regularly" (alternate letters).

## Punctuation That Matters

Most punctuation in a cryptic clue is there for the surface and can be ignored. A few marks carry real meaning.

![What punctuation means in a cryptic clue: a question mark warns of a loose or cryptic definition, an exclamation mark often signals an &lit, an ellipsis links clues together, while capital letters, dashes and colons are usually there only for the surface](/images/blog/cryptic-crossword-glossary/punctuation.webp)

### Question mark

A **question mark** at the end of a clue warns that something is not quite literal. It often signals a cryptic definition, a definition by example or a playful stretch in the wordplay. It is the setter's way of saying "I know this is a bit cheeky".

### Exclamation mark

An **exclamation mark** at the end of a clue often signals an &lit, where the whole clue doubles as the definition. It can also simply mark a joke. Either way, it invites you to read the clue as a whole.

### Ellipsis

An **ellipsis** (...) at the end of one clue and the start of the next shows that the two clues are linked. Usually the surface runs on, but each clue still solves on its own. Occasionally, one clue's answer is needed to solve the other.

### Capital letters

**Capital letters** in a clue mean nothing and are often a trap. A capitalised word like "Turkey" might be the bird, and "Bill" might be an invoice. Setters are allowed to capitalise a word to mislead you, but they will not lower-case a proper noun that needs a capital.

### Dashes and colons

**Dashes and colons** usually exist only to make the surface read smoothly. They often mark the boundary between definition and wordplay, but you should not rely on that. In "Breakfast fibre: label, almost (4)", the colon happens to sit exactly at the split.

## Grid and Puzzle Terms

These terms describe the puzzle itself rather than the clues.

### Setter

The **setter**, or **compiler**, is the person who writes the crossword. In many British newspapers, setters publish under a pseudonym; The Times is a notable exception, where puzzles are unsigned.

### Solver

The **solver** is the person doing the puzzle. Setters write for the solver, and the best clues are said to be tough but fair: hard to see, but obviously right once seen.

### Grid

The **grid** is the pattern of squares the answers go into. Most daily cryptics use a symmetrical grid of 15 by 15 squares.

### Light

A **light** is an answer entry in the grid: the run of squares that a single answer fills. Setters and puzzle editors use the word when they talk about a grid’s answers as a whole.

### Checked letters

**Checked letters**, also called **checkers** or **crossers**, are squares shared between an across answer and a down answer. Each one you solve gives you a free letter in another answer.

### Unches

**Unches** are unchecked letters: squares that belong to only one answer. In a typical cryptic grid, roughly half the letters of each answer are unches, which is why you need to solve the clue itself rather than rely on crossing letters.

### Blocked grid

A **blocked grid** uses black squares to separate answers, like the crosswords in most daily newspapers. It is the format most solvers learn on.

### Barred grid

A **barred grid** uses thick lines between squares instead of black squares, so almost every square is checked. Barred grids are used for advanced puzzles and allow more obscure words, because the extra checking makes them fair.

### Quick cryptic

A **quick cryptic** is an easier cryptic crossword designed for beginners, with fewer clues, more common words and simpler wordplay. Several British newspapers publish one alongside their main cryptic.

### Prize puzzle

A **prize puzzle** is a crossword, often harder than the daily, whose solution is withheld until a later date so that solvers can send in entries for a prize.

### Nina

A **nina** is a hidden message or theme in the grid itself, for example a word spelled out along the edges. The name comes from the cartoonist Al Hirschfeld, who hid his daughter Nina's name in his drawings.

### Theme

A **theme** is a link running through several answers in the same puzzle, such as rivers, Shakespeare plays or a famous anniversary. Spotting the theme early can unlock the remaining clues.

### Par

In the Minute Cryptic app, **par** is a target score for each clue, borrowed from golf. According to the app's own guide, par is set from the answer's length: roughly half the letters you would expect to get from crossings in a full grid, plus a hint. Lower is better.

## Setting Styles and Solver Slang

The last group covers the culture around cryptics: the arguments setters have and the slang solvers use.

### Ximenean

**Ximenean** describes a strict style of clue-writing, named after the pseudonym of Derrick Macnutt, who set crosswords for The Observer as "Ximenes". A Ximenean clue has exactly one definition, fair wordplay and no unnecessary words. His 1966 book *Ximenes on the Art of the Crossword* set out the rules.

### Libertarian

**Libertarian** describes a looser style that bends Ximenean rules for the sake of wit or a great surface. The Guardian's Araucaria is the setter most often named as its champion. Many modern puzzles sit somewhere between the two styles.

### Afrit's Injunction

**Afrit's Injunction** is the most famous rule of fair clueing, from the setter Afrit (Alistair Ferguson Ritchie): "You need not mean what you say, but you must say what you mean." In other words, the surface may mislead, but the clue must still literally lead to the answer.

### Crosswordese

**Crosswordese** means words that appear far more often in crosswords than in everyday life, because their letters are useful to setters. Short words for birds and animals, such as ERNE (a sea eagle), EMU and GNU, are classic examples.

### Chestnut

A **chestnut** is a clue or trick so familiar that experienced solvers spot it instantly, such as "flower" meaning a river. Chestnuts are useful for beginners, but setters try not to rely on them.

### Penny drop

The **penny drop** is the moment a clue suddenly makes sense. It is the reason people do cryptic crosswords: the sharp shift from baffled to delighted that no ordinary crossword can match.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does "fodder" mean in a cryptic crossword?

Fodder is the raw material the wordplay works on. In an anagram, it is the word or words you rearrange, such as "carthorse" in "Players from carthorse running loose (9)", which gives ORCHESTRA. In a hidden-word clue, it is the phrase that contains the answer.

### What does par mean in Minute Cryptic?

In the Minute Cryptic app, par is a target score for each clue, borrowed from golf. The app's guide says it is based on the answer's length, roughly half the letters you would get from crossing answers in a full grid plus a hint. Scoring at or below par is the goal.

### What does an exclamation mark mean in a cryptic clue?

An exclamation mark at the end of a cryptic clue often signals an &lit, a clue where the whole sentence is both the definition and the wordplay. It can also simply mark a joke. Either way, read the clue as one complete idea.

### What does a question mark mean in a cryptic clue?

A question mark warns that something in the clue is not literal. It often marks a cryptic definition, a definition by example such as "Thames, perhaps", or a playful stretch in the wordplay. Treat it as a hint to think laterally.

### What is the difference between Ximenean and Libertarian clues?

Ximenean clues follow strict rules: one definition, fair wordplay and no spare words, as set out by the setter Ximenes in 1966. Libertarian clues relax those rules for wit or surface. Most daily puzzles are broadly Ximenean, with occasional libertarian flourishes.

### What are unches in a crossword?

Unches are unchecked letters: squares that belong to only one answer, so no crossing clue helps you fill them. In a typical cryptic grid, roughly half of each answer's letters are unches, which is why every clue needs to be solvable on its own.
