A minute cryptic gives you one clue a day and a few letters to fill. That makes it the perfect training ground for cryptic crosswords, and also a fine way to lose ten minutes staring at seven words.

These 15 tips are what we have learned from writing and explaining a daily clue. Several of them come with numbers. We measured patterns across our library of **1,004 explained clues**, such as where the definition sits, which indicators turn up most and how harder clues differ from easy ones.

**What is a minute cryptic?** A minute cryptic is a single cryptic crossword clue, released daily, designed to be solved in a minute or two. Like any cryptic clue, it combines a definition of the answer with wordplay that builds the same answer another way. The format was popularised by the Minute Cryptic app and book.

This article adds data-backed tips to our complete guide on [how to solve Minute Cryptic](/how-to-play-minute-cryptic), which covers the full step-by-step method. The tips work for the Minute Cryptic app, for [our own daily clue](/minute-cryptic-today) and for any short cryptic. dailycryptic.org is independent and is not affiliated with the Minute Cryptic app or book.

## Tip 1: Find the Definition First

Every cryptic clue has a definition at one end. Before you try any wordplay, test the first word or two and the last word or two as possible definitions. One of them will be a plain synonym of the answer.

In our library, the split between the two ends is close to even. Among the 755 clues with a single definition, it sat at the start 396 times (52%) and at the end 359 times (48%). So never assume, and always check both ends.

> **Players from carthorse running loose (9)** — *puzzle of 31 August 2026*
> "Players" at the start is the definition. The rest is wordplay: CARTHORSE rearranged gives **ORCHESTRA**.

## Tip 3: Count the Letters

The number in brackets is your most reliable piece of information. It rules out most of the synonyms you think of.

In our library, answers range from 3 to 9 letters, and **78% are four, five or six letters long**. Five letters is the single most common length (316 clues), followed by four (251) and six (220). Longer answers are rarer and tend to appear in harder clues.

| Difficulty | Clues | Average answer length |
|---|---|---|
| Easy | 354 | 4.9 letters |
| Medium | 385 | 5.2 letters |
| Hard | 265 | 5.8 letters |

Pay attention to the shape of the enumeration, too. A comma, as in (4,4), means two words, so you are looking for a phrase such as TALL TALE. A hyphen, as in (5-3), means a hyphenated answer. When a clue's surface suggests one word but the enumeration shows two, that mismatch is often the key to the clue.

## Tip 5: Check for a Hidden Word Before Anything Else

Hidden-word clues are the fastest to solve, so test for one first. Run the letters of the clue together and scan for the answer length. It takes ten seconds and catches a whole category of clues.

> **Waterway hidden in African Alps (5)** — *puzzle of 10 October 2026*
> Run the letters together: AFRICANALPS. Inside: afri**CAN AL**ps gives **CANAL**.

The giveaway is often a slightly odd phrase. "African Alps" is not something you would normally say, and that is a hint that the words are there for their letters.

## Tip 6: Say It Out Loud

If the clue contains "we hear", "reportedly" or "sounds like", stop reading and start listening. Homophone clues only work in your ear.

> **Hidden store that sounds like money (5)** — *puzzle of 22 August 2026*
> Say CASH aloud and you hear **CACHE**, a hidden store.

Read the possible answers aloud, too. If two words sound the same, the definition tells you which spelling to enter.

> **Flatfish we hear is a location (6)** — *puzzle of 19 October 2026*
> PLACE (a location) sounds like **PLAICE**, a flatfish. The definition at the start tells you which of the two spellings goes in the grid.

In our library, homophone clues put the definition first 76% of the time, so the first word or two is usually the spelling you need.

## Tip 7: Count the Fodder for Anagrams

For a suspected anagram, look for a word or phrase whose letters add up to exactly the answer length, sitting next to an indicator like "mixed", "broken" or "wild". If the count matches, write the letters out and rearrange them on paper.

> **Christmas decoration when enlist is reorganised (6)** — *puzzle of 30 July 2026*
> ENLIST has six letters, and "reorganised" is the indicator. Rearranged, it gives **TINSEL**.

💡 **Pro tip:** write the fodder letters in a circle rather than a line. It breaks the pattern of the original word and makes new words easier to see.

## Tip 8: Ignore the Story

The surface reading is designed to mislead. A clue about soup, horses or Christmas probably has nothing to do with soup, horses or Christmas. Read each word for what it could mean on its own, not for what it means in the sentence.

> **Thin soup: both clutching resistance (5)** — *puzzle of 6 August 2026*
> The story is about a bowl of soup. The wordplay is BOTH holding R (resistance), giving **BROTH**.

## Tip 9: Treat Small Words With Suspicion

Short, ordinary words are often letters in disguise. "Nothing" can be O. "At home" can be IN. "Resistance" can be R. In our library, R alone appeared 22 times as an abbreviation, standing for resistance, right or river.

> **Moan: gran holding nothing (5)** — *puzzle of 5 October 2026*
> "Nothing" is O. GRAN holding O gives **GROAN**.

Our [abbreviations reference](/cryptic-abbreviations) lists them, and [why love is O](/blog/cryptic-crossword-abbreviations) explains which ones to learn first.

## Tip 10: Learn the Language of Heads and Tails

Deletion clues talk about bodies: a word can lose its **head** (first letter), its **tail** or **end** (last letter) or its **heart** (middle). Once you know the vocabulary, these clues become mechanical.

> **Warmth from wheat losing its head (4)** — *puzzle of 23 July 2026*
> WHEAT minus its head W gives **HEAT**.

"Almost" and "nearly" also mean the last letter goes, as in "Breakfast fibre: label, almost (4)", where BRAND loses its D to give BRAN.

"Heartless" takes out the middle. In "Holy man, heartless, is just (4)", from 28 July 2026, FAKIR loses its central K to leave **FAIR**. In our library, deletion clues put the definition at the end 74% of the time, so the first words are usually the word you start from.

## Tip 11: Use Hints One Step at a Time

Hints are a learning tool, not a defeat, but take them one at a time. On our daily clue, the hints are ordered from least to most revealing:

1. **Where the definition is.** Often this alone unlocks the clue.
2. **The type of wordplay.** Now you know what operation to try.
3. **A nudge on the wordplay itself.** For example, which word to rearrange.
4. **The first letter.**

After each hint, have another go before asking for the next. The Minute Cryptic app has a similar philosophy: it sets a "par" for each clue, a target number of hints and letter reveals, so using fewer is something to aim for rather than something to avoid entirely.

## Tip 12: Check Both Halves Before You Enter

A correct answer satisfies the definition **and** the wordplay. If your answer only fits one half, it is probably wrong, even if it feels right. Run the wordplay forwards one last time and make sure every word of the clue has been used.

This habit saves more streaks than any other tip. It catches the near-miss synonyms that fit the definition but not the letters.

For example, in "Royal when lager is poured back (5)", both REGAL and ROYAL fit the definition and have five letters. Only REGAL is LAGER written backwards, so only REGAL satisfies both halves.

## Tip 13: Keep a Personal Indicator List

Every time an explanation surprises you, write the indicator down. "Swallows" for a container, "returned" for a reversal, "endlessly" for a deletion. After a few weeks you will have a list tuned to the setter you solve most often.

Organise the list by device rather than alphabetically. When you meet a new clue, you can then run down the "reversal" column or the "container" column and check whether any of the clue's words appear there.

A simple notebook works, as does a note on your phone. The format matters less than the habit: one new indicator a day adds up to well over a hundred in a few months.

## Tip 15: Read the Explanation Even When You Win

Solving a clue by instinct is satisfying, but it teaches you little unless you know *why* it worked. Read the full explanation every day, including on days you solve it in seconds. You will often find you got the right answer for a slightly wrong reason.

Every clue in our archive has a worked explanation, and most now include a step-by-step breakdown of the definition, the wordplay and the setter's misdirection.

## Five Mistakes That Slow Solvers Down

Tips tell you what to do. It is just as useful to know what wastes time. These are the habits we see most often in solvers who are stuck at the same level.

### 1. Reading the clue as a sentence

The surface is a decoy. If you spend your first minute wondering how soup relates to resistance, you are solving the setter's story instead of the clue. Break the sentence into pieces straight away.

### 2. Assuming the definition comes first

Many beginners always try the first word as the definition, because that is where definitions sit in ordinary crosswords. In our library, the definition was at the end in nearly half of all single-definition clues, and in almost three quarters of deletion clues.

### 3. Forcing an anagram

Not every clue is an anagram. If you cannot find an indicator next to a word with the right number of letters, stop shuffling and try another device. Anagrams made up 15% of our clues, so roughly six clues in seven need something else.

### 4. Ignoring the small words

Words like "a", "in", "on" and "about" are easy to skim past, but each one might be a letter, a link or an indicator. "About" alone can mean C, CA or RE, or tell you to put one word around another.

### 5. Entering an answer that fits only one half

The definition says "fruit" and you think of PEAR, so you type it in. But if the wordplay does not also give PEAR, the answer is wrong. A minute spent checking saves the frustration of a broken streak.

## A 60-Second Routine

Put the tips together and you get a routine you can run on any clue:

1. **Read the enumeration** and note the answer length.
2. **Test both ends** for a definition.
3. **Scan for a hidden word** across the whole clue.
4. **Find the indicator**, then decide the device.
5. **Build the answer**, then check it against both halves.

If you are still stuck after a minute, take one hint and run the routine again.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you get better at Minute Cryptic?

Practise one clue a day, read every explanation and learn the common indicators. Start by finding the definition at one end of the clue, then work out the wordplay. Keeping a list of indicators that surprise you is one of the fastest ways to improve.

### Where is the definition in a minute cryptic clue?

Always at the start or the end, never in the middle. In our library of 1,004 clues, single definitions sat at the start 52% of the time and at the end 48%. Hidden-word clues put the definition first 94% of the time, while deletion clues put it last 74% of the time.

### What does par mean in Minute Cryptic?

In the Minute Cryptic app, par is a target score for each clue, borrowed from golf. According to the app's guide, it is based on the answer's length: roughly half the letters you would expect from crossings in a full grid, plus a hint. Lower scores are better.

### Is it cheating to use hints?

No. Hints are designed for learning. Use them one at a time, and try the clue again after each one. On our daily clue, the first hint tells you where the definition is, which is often enough to unlock the whole clue.

### What are the most common clue types in Minute Cryptic?

Every standard cryptic device appears. In our own library of 1,004 clues, charades and double definitions were the most common (165 and 160 clues), followed by anagrams (150) and deletions (120). Easy clues favour charades and double definitions; hard clues use more containers.

### How long should a minute cryptic take?

The format is designed for a minute or two, but there is no time limit. Beginners often take five to ten minutes at first. Speed comes from recognising indicators quickly, which improves with daily practice and reading explanations.
