Most cryptic wordplay is something you *see* — letters rearranged, hidden inside a phrase, or stacked end to end. Homophone clues are the exception. They work on your *ear*. The wordplay hands you a word that sounds exactly like the answer but is spelled differently, and your job is to hear the connection.

That small shift — from reading to listening — is why homophones trip up so many solvers. You can stare at the letters all day and never spot it, because the trick was never on the page. It was in the sound.

<Callout>
**The one-line rule:** in a homophone clue, one part of the clue is a word that *sounds like* the answer, flagged by a word meaning "spoken" or "heard". Solve it by saying candidates out loud, not by studying the spelling.
</Callout>

## How a Homophone Clue Works

Like every cryptic clue, a homophone clue has two halves: a straight **definition** and some **wordplay**. In this clue type, the wordplay is a word (or words) that sounds the same as the answer, plus an **indicator** telling you to listen.

Take this example:

> **Animal is naked, we hear (4)**

![Anatomy of a homophone clue: the definition "Animal" points to BEAR, the soundalike word "naked" gives BARE, and the indicator "we hear" tells you to listen — BARE sounds like BEAR](/images/homophone-anatomy.svg)

Break it into three pieces:

- **"Animal"** is the definition — it points at the answer, BEAR.
- **"naked"** is the soundalike word — it means BARE.
- **"we hear"** is the homophone indicator — it tells you the wordplay is about sound.

BARE and BEAR are spelled nothing alike, but they sound identical. Say "naked" → BARE → and you *hear* BEAR, the animal. That is the whole mechanism: the clue routes you to the answer through a word that rhymes with it perfectly.

## Homophone Indicators: The Words That Give It Away

You will almost never solve a homophone clue without first spotting the indicator. These are the little signposts that mean "this is about sound". Train your eye to flag them the moment they appear, exactly the way you already flag anagram or [cryptic indicators](/cryptic-indicators) for other clue types.

Common homophone indicators include:

- **we hear**, **I hear**, **heard**
- **reportedly**, **said**, **so to speak**
- **aloud**, **out loud**, **spoken**
- **on the radio**, **broadcast**, **audibly**
- **by the sound of it**, **it's said**

Any word that points to speaking, listening, or broadcasting is a candidate. When you see one, stop solving the clue literally and start reading it for sound instead.

## Homophone Pairs Setters Love

English is full of words that sound identical but mean completely different things, and cryptic setters mine that seam constantly. A handful of pairs turn up again and again, so it pays to know them on sight.

![Six common homophone pairs used in cryptic crosswords: ALOUD and ALLOWED, KNIGHT and NIGHT, FLOUR and FLOWER, HEARD and HERD, SEA and SEE, BARE and BEAR](/images/homophone-common-pairs.svg)

Notice how each pair gives the setter two very different definitions to hide behind. KNIGHT can be clued as a chess piece or a medieval warrior, while its twin NIGHT is an evening. FLOUR belongs in a kitchen; FLOWER in a garden. The setter picks whichever meaning is *not* the answer and dresses the wordplay around it — so the surface reading sends you the wrong way while the sound sends you the right way.

## Solve by Ear: Read the Clue Aloud

Here is the single most useful habit for this clue type: **say it out loud.** Homophones live in speech, so the fastest way to test one is to voice it. Read the suspected soundalike word, then run through answers of the right length that share its sound.

There is a catch, though. Some near-homophones only *look* like they match, and some genuinely obscure words are hard to pronounce from the page. When you are unsure whether two words truly sound the same — or you have never heard a word spoken at all — it helps to let something read them to you. Pasting a candidate pair into a text-to-speech tool like [AnySpeech](https://anyspeech.io/) and listening back removes the guesswork: if the two words come out identical, you have a homophone; if they don't, keep looking.

This is doubly handy for solvers who are learning English, or who simply want to hear a clue read cleanly aloud without their own inner voice biasing the pronunciation. Letting a neutral voice speak the words is often the quickest way to confirm — or rule out — a homophone.

<Callout>
**Try it now:** take the answer you suspect and the soundalike word from the clue, and hear them side by side. If your ear says "same word", you've cracked it.
</Callout>

## Watch Out: Accents Change Everything

Homophones are not universal. Two words that sound identical in one accent can be clearly different in another, and this is the most common reason a homophone clue feels "unfair".

British cryptic setters generally write for **Received Pronunciation** — the standard British accent — so their homophones assume that pronunciation. A classic trap is the non-rhotic accent, where the "r" at the end of a word goes quiet. In RP, "father" and "farther" are homophones; in many American or Scottish accents they are not. Likewise "paw", "pour" and "poor" can all collapse into one sound in RP while staying distinct elsewhere.

If a homophone clue looks wrong to you, try it in a different accent before deciding the setter made a mistake. Nine times out of ten the sound works — just not in *your* accent.

## Put It Into Practice

Homophone clues reward the same thing every cryptic device does: spotting the signal, then trusting it. Flag the indicator, isolate the soundalike word, say it aloud, and let your ear do the rest.
