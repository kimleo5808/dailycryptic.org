Ask a crossword fan in New York and a crossword fan in London what a crossword is, and you may get two different answers. One will describe a grid packed with short words and trivia clues. The other will describe sentences that seem to make no sense until, suddenly, they do.

Both are crosswords. They share a grid, numbered clues and the pleasure of filling in the last square. But they ask for different skills, and knowing the difference will save you from bouncing off the wrong one, or from assuming you are bad at both.

**What is a regular crossword?** A regular crossword, often called an American-style or "straight" crossword, gives one plain clue for each answer: a definition, a fill-in-the-blank or a piece of trivia. Every square in the grid belongs to both an across and a down answer, so crossing letters help constantly.

**What is a cryptic crossword?** A cryptic crossword gives clues that work twice. Each clue contains a definition of the answer plus wordplay, such as an anagram or a hidden word, that builds the same answer another way. Grids are more open, so each clue usually has to be solved on its own merits.

## The Short Answer: Side by Side

| Dimension | Regular crossword | Cryptic crossword |
|---|---|---|
| What a clue contains | One meaning or fact | A definition **plus** wordplay |
| What a clue reads like | A definition or trivia question | A sentence that seems to mean something else |
| Answer length shown? | No, you count the squares | Yes, in brackets: (5) or (4,4) |
| Answers allowed | Words, phrases, abbreviations, partial phrases | Dictionary words and phrases |
| Grid | Every letter is crossed | About half of each answer is crossed |
| Main skill | Vocabulary and general knowledge | Spotting wordplay devices |
| Checking your answer | Crossing letters | The wordplay itself confirms it |
| Typical home | United States | UK, Ireland, Australia, NZ, Canada, India, South Africa |
| Difficulty signal | Day of the week (NYT) | Paper, setter and "quick" vs main puzzle |

The rest of this guide explains each difference, then helps you decide which to try first.

## Difference 1: What a Clue Contains

A regular clue has one job: point to the answer. It might be a synonym ("Moan"), a fill-in-the-blank ("___ and groan") or a fact ("Panama ___").

A cryptic clue has two jobs. It points to the answer with a definition at one end, and it builds the answer with wordplay in the rest. Solving it means finding where one part ends and the other begins. Our guide to [cryptic clue types](/cryptic-clue-types) covers the eight devices the wordplay can use.

## Difference 2: How Clues Read

Regular clues read like dictionary entries or quiz questions. They are terse and literal, with one exception: a question mark in an American clue signals a pun.

Cryptic clues read like ordinary sentences, often with a little story: a carthorse running loose, a hobo eating, a waiter dropping a glass. That story is called the [surface reading](/blog/surface-reading-cryptic-clues), and it is deliberately misleading. In a cryptic, the clue that reads most naturally is often the hardest to crack.

## Difference 3: Letter Counts

Cryptic clues tell you the answer length in brackets, including how many words: (4,4) means two four-letter words and (5-3) a hyphenated answer.

Regular American crosswords do not show letter counts. You count the squares, and multi-word answers appear without spaces, so you might not know that SEEYA is two words until you solve it.

## Difference 4: What Counts as an Answer

American-style grids accept almost anything that fits: abbreviations (flagged with "Abbr."), partial phrases, brand names, foreign words and short "crosswordese" fillers that keep the grid tightly interlocked.

Cryptic answers are usually dictionary words or established phrases. Because each answer must be clued with fair wordplay, setters avoid fragments that cannot be defined.

The difference shows up in the short words. American grids lean on a familiar cast of three- and four-letter fillers, such as ERA, ALOE, OREO and ERNE, because a fully interlocked grid needs a lot of them. Cryptic grids have fewer short answers, and each one still needs a proper definition and wordplay.

## Difference 5: Grid Design

This difference explains many of the others.

![Two grid styles compared: in an American-style grid every white square belongs to both an across and a down answer, so every letter is checked; in a British cryptic grid about half the squares in each answer are unchecked, belonging to only one answer](/images/blog/cryptic-vs-regular-crossword/grids.webp)

In an **American-style grid**, every white square belongs to both an across answer and a down answer. Every letter is "checked", so if you know one answer, it helps two others.

In a typical **British cryptic grid**, about half the squares in each answer are unchecked. Solvers call them "unches". You cannot rely on crossing letters to finish an answer, which is why every cryptic clue must be solvable on its own. The trade-off is that the clues themselves carry much more information.

## Difference 6: The Skills You Use

Regular crosswords reward **vocabulary and general knowledge**: film titles, sports names, geography, old song lyrics and the stock of short words that grids depend on.

Cryptics reward **pattern recognition**. Knowledge still helps, but the core skill is spotting devices: seeing that "reorganised" means rearrange, that "nothing" can be the letter O, or that "African Alps" hides a word. Our [abbreviations](/cryptic-abbreviations) and [indicator](/cryptic-indicators) guides cover the vocabulary these patterns use.

## Difference 7: How You Know You Are Right

In a regular crossword, you often know an answer is right only when the crossing answers agree with it.

In a cryptic, the clue checks itself. When the wordplay builds exactly the word the definition describes, you know you are right, even with no crossing letters. That moment of certainty, when a nonsense sentence suddenly resolves, is the main reason people get hooked on cryptics.

## Difference 8: How Difficulty Works

The New York Times crossword famously gets harder through the week, from a gentle Monday to a fiendish Saturday, with a larger Sunday puzzle of roughly midweek difficulty. Difficulty comes from vaguer clues and harder vocabulary.

British papers usually signal difficulty differently. Many publish a **quick cryptic** for beginners alongside the main daily cryptic, and the main puzzle varies with the setter rather than the day. Harder cryptics use subtler wordplay and less familiar indicators rather than more obscure answers.

## Difference 9: History and Geography

The crossword itself is American. The first one, designed by Arthur Wynne, appeared in the New York World on 21 December 1913. It used plain definition clues, and that style still dominates in the United States.

Cryptic crosswords grew up in British newspapers in the 1920s and 1930s, as setters such as Torquemada at The Observer began writing clues that played with words rather than simply defining them. Today cryptics are the main style in the UK, Ireland, Australia, New Zealand, India and South Africa, and they are popular in Canada. American publications run them occasionally, often as variety puzzles.

## One Clue, Solved Both Ways

Here is what the difference feels like in practice. Take the answer GROAN and imagine solving it in each style with no crossing letters.

### As a regular clue: "Moan (5)"

You list five-letter words for moan: GROAN, WHINE, GRIPE, GRUMP, perhaps BLEAT. All are plausible. Without crossing letters, you cannot choose between them, so you move on and come back when the grid gives you a letter or two. The clue alone is not enough.

### As a cryptic clue: "Moan: gran holding nothing (5)"

You still think of GROAN, WHINE and GRIPE for "Moan". But now the rest of the clue gives you a second route. "Gran holding nothing" means GRAN with O inside: GR-O-AN. Only one of your candidates matches, and the clue has proved it. You can write GROAN in with confidence, even in an empty grid.

That is the essential trade. A cryptic clue asks you to do more work per clue, but it gives you more certainty in return. A regular clue is quicker to read, but it often needs the grid's help to be sure.

## Four Common Misconceptions About Cryptics

People who have never tried a cryptic often avoid them for reasons that are not quite true.

### "You need to be a genius"

Cryptic clues follow a small set of rules, and there are only about eight main devices to learn. Most solvers become comfortable within a few weeks of regular practice. Speed and confidence come from familiarity, not brilliance.

### "You need to know Latin and British slang"

A few abbreviations come from Latin, such as C for "about" from *circa*, and some are British, such as cricket terms. But they are a short, learnable list, and easier puzzles lean on the most familiar ones. Our article on [how cryptic abbreviations work](/blog/cryptic-crossword-abbreviations) covers the essentials.

### "Regular crosswords are the easy version"

A Saturday New York Times puzzle is as hard as most cryptics, just in a different way. Difficulty in American puzzles comes from vague clues and wide general knowledge, which can be just as testing as wordplay.

### "You have to solve in order"

In either style, you should start wherever you have a foothold. In a cryptic, that often means looking for the clue types you find easiest, such as hidden words or anagrams, and building from there.

## Which Is Harder?

Neither, exactly. They are hard in different ways.

A regular crossword can stop you dead if you do not know a fact: no amount of reasoning will tell you an actor's middle name. A cryptic can stop you dead if you do not spot a device: no amount of general knowledge will help if you miss that "reorganised" means rearrange.

Most people find cryptics harder **at first**, because the clues seem to break the rules of language. After a few weeks of learning the devices, many find them easier than hard regular puzzles, because every clue contains its own proof.

## When to Start With a Regular Crossword

- You enjoy trivia and general knowledge.
- You want quick wins: a Monday NYT puzzle or a small mini crossword can be finished in minutes.
- You prefer puzzles where the clues mean exactly what they say.

## When to Start With a Cryptic

- You like wordplay, puns, anagrams and riddles.
- You enjoy NYT Connections' purple group, which uses many of the same tricks. Our [analysis of 660 Connections puzzles](/blog/nyt-connections-purple-category) found that 77% of purple groups are wordplay.
- You want a puzzle that rewards reasoning more than recall.

The easiest way to start is with a single clue a day, such as a [daily minute cryptic](/minute-cryptic-today), rather than a full grid. One clue at a time lets you learn each device without the pressure of a whole puzzle.

## Where to Find Each Style

You do not need a newspaper subscription to try either style, but it helps to know where each lives.

**Regular crosswords** are everywhere in the United States. The New York Times publishes a daily puzzle and a small "Mini", and USA Today, the Los Angeles Times and The New Yorker all run American-style crosswords. Most puzzle apps default to this style.

**Cryptic crosswords** are a fixture of British papers such as The Guardian, The Times, The Daily Telegraph and the Financial Times, many of which also publish a quick cryptic for beginners. Australian, New Zealand, Irish and Indian papers carry them too.

**Single-clue cryptics** are the newest format: one cryptic clue a day, solvable in a minute or two. The Minute Cryptic app popularised the idea, and our own [daily minute cryptic](/minute-cryptic-today) works the same way, with hints and a full explanation for every clue. For a beginner, this is the gentlest way in.

## How Skills Transfer

The two styles help each other more than you might expect.

Regular solvers already have the most important cryptic skill: a large vocabulary of synonyms. Every cryptic definition is a regular clue in disguise. Cryptic solvers, in turn, become very good at spotting the punning "?" clues in American puzzles, because they are used to reading words in more than one way.

If you solve one style, the other is a natural next step rather than a different hobby.

## How to Explain the Difference to a Friend

If someone asks you what a cryptic crossword is, these one-line explanations work well:

- **For a regular crossword fan:** "Every clue has a normal crossword clue inside it, plus a puzzle that spells out the same answer."
- **For a word-game fan:** "It's like the purple group in Connections, but every clue tells you which trick it's using."
- **For anyone:** "Each clue is a riddle with a built-in answer key."

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between a cryptic and a regular crossword?

A regular crossword clue gives one plain meaning or fact. A cryptic clue gives a definition plus wordplay that builds the same answer another way. Cryptic clues also show the answer length, and cryptic grids have fewer crossing letters, so each clue must be solved on its own.

### Are cryptic crosswords harder than regular crosswords?

They are harder at first, because the clues seem to break the rules of language. Once you learn the main devices, such as anagrams, hidden words and charades, many solvers find cryptics easier than hard regular puzzles, because the wordplay confirms each answer.

### Why are cryptic crosswords British?

Cryptic crosswords developed in British newspapers in the 1920s and 1930s, when setters such as Torquemada at The Observer began writing clues built on wordplay. The style spread through the Commonwealth, while American papers kept the plain-definition style of the first crossword, published in 1913.

### Can I solve a cryptic clue without any crossing letters?

Yes. Every fair cryptic clue contains enough information to solve it on its own: a definition and wordplay that both lead to the answer. That is why single-clue puzzles like a daily minute cryptic work, while a single regular clue often has many possible answers.

### Should a beginner start with a cryptic or a regular crossword?

If you enjoy trivia and quick wins, start with a regular crossword. If you enjoy puns, anagrams and riddles, start with a cryptic, ideally one clue a day rather than a full grid. Both build vocabulary, and the skills transfer in both directions.

### Does the New York Times publish cryptic crosswords?

The New York Times mainly publishes American-style crosswords, but it has run cryptic crosswords occasionally as variety puzzles. Most daily cryptics come from British, Irish, Australian, New Zealand, Canadian and Indian publications, and from single-clue formats online.
